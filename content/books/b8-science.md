## Where does the food come from?

Think about your last meal. Perhaps it was banku with okro stew, or rice and beans, or kelewele. Every bit of it can be traced back to a **plant**: the maize in the banku, the rice, the plantain, even the fish, which ate smaller creatures that ate tiny floating plants.

But where did the plant get *its* food? A cassava plant does not eat. Nobody feeds it. Yet it grows from a stick pushed into the ground to a bush with heavy roots full of starch.

The answer is one of the most important processes on Earth: **photosynthesis**. Plants make their own food out of air, water and sunlight.

And there is a second process, running in every living cell including yours right now, that releases the energy stored in that food: **respiration**.

This book is about both. By the end you will be able to write the word equation for each, explain what a leaf needs, test a leaf for starch, and say why neither process could exist for long without the other.
===
## Living things need energy

Every living thing uses **energy** all the time:

- to **move** – a fish swimming, a goat running, your heart beating
- to **grow** and repair – new cells, healing a cut
- to **keep warm** – your body stays at about 37 °C even on a cool harmattan night
- to **send messages** – nerves carrying signals to your brain
- to **make new substances** – proteins, enzymes, the walls of plant cells

Where does the energy come from? From **food**. Food contains **chemical energy** locked up in substances such as **glucose** (a sugar). When a cell breaks glucose down, the energy is released and the cell can use it.

Animals get glucose by eating. Plants cannot eat, so they must make glucose themselves. That is photosynthesis. Both plants and animals then release the energy by respiration.

So the two big questions are: **how is glucose made?** and **how is its energy released?**

## What is photosynthesis?

@image https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/55/Photosynthesis_en.svg/960px-Photosynthesis_en.svg.png | The inputs and outputs of photosynthesis: light, water and carbon dioxide in; glucose and oxygen out | At09kg, Wattcle, Nefronus · CC BY-SA 4.0 | https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Photosynthesis_en.svg

**Photosynthesis** is the process by which green plants make glucose from carbon dioxide and water, using the energy of sunlight.

The name tells you what it means: *photo* = light, *synthesis* = making. Making with light.

It happens mainly in the **leaves**, inside tiny green structures called **chloroplasts**, which contain the green pigment **chlorophyll**.

Here is the whole thing in one sentence:

> The plant takes in **carbon dioxide** from the air and **water** from the soil; using **light energy** trapped by **chlorophyll**, it joins them to make **glucose**, and **oxygen** is given off.

Glucose is the plant's food. Some is used straight away for energy; some is turned into **starch** and stored; some is used to build the plant itself: cellulose for cell walls, and with minerals from the soil, proteins and other substances.

Every time you breathe in, the oxygen comes from this process.
===
## The word equation for photosynthesis

Scientists summarise a chemical change in a **word equation**. On the left are the substances that go in (the **reactants**); on the right, what comes out (the **products**).

> **carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen**

And because this change needs energy and a helper, we write them above or beside the arrow:

> carbon dioxide + water —(light energy, chlorophyll)→ glucose + oxygen

Say it aloud until you can write it from memory. In an exam, you must get these right:

- the **two reactants**: carbon dioxide and water
- the **two products**: glucose and oxygen
- the **two conditions**: light and chlorophyll

Light and chlorophyll are not used up, so they do not appear as reactants; they make the reaction possible.

## What a plant needs: light

Light is the **energy source**. Without it, no photosynthesis happens at all.

Evidence you can see:

- A plant grown in a dark room becomes pale and spindly, and eventually dies, even if it is watered.
- Grass under a mat or a board turns yellow in a few days. Lift the board and it is pale where the light was blocked.
- Plants on a windowsill lean towards the window.
- Farmers space maize plants so each gets enough sun; crowded plants give a poor harvest.

Sunlight is strongest around midday, which is when photosynthesis is fastest. On a cloudy day in the rainy season, plants make less glucose than on a bright day.

In Ghana we rarely lack light; our plants' problem is more often water. But a seedling under a thick tree canopy is light-starved, which is why the forest floor has few plants and why farmers clear and thin trees.

## What a plant needs: water

Water is one of the two **reactants**. It enters the plant through the **roots**, travels up the stem in narrow tubes called **xylem**, and reaches the leaves.

Only a small part of the water taken up is used in photosynthesis. Most of it evaporates from the leaves through tiny pores. This evaporation, called **transpiration**, pulls more water up from the roots, like sucking through a straw, and also keeps the leaf cool.

When water is short:

- the plant closes its leaf pores to save water
- but closing them also shuts out carbon dioxide
- so photosynthesis slows or stops
- the leaves wilt and the plant stops growing

That is why a maize field in a dry spell stops growing even though the sun is shining. And it is why irrigation matters: around the Volta, farms watered from the lake can grow crops in the dry season when rain-fed farms cannot.

Water is also what carries dissolved **minerals** from the soil into the plant, such as nitrates for making proteins.
===
## What a plant needs: carbon dioxide

Carbon dioxide (CO₂) is the other reactant. It is a gas, and only about **0.04 %** of the air, yet plants pull enough of it in to build every leaf, stem and root.

It enters the leaf through tiny openings called **stomata** (one is a **stoma**), mostly on the underside of the leaf. Inside, it dissolves in the thin film of water around the cells and diffuses into the chloroplasts.

Where does the carbon dioxide come from? From **respiration** by animals, plants and microbes; from **burning** wood, charcoal, petrol and gas; and from **decay**.

Here is a surprising fact: most of the **mass** of a tree does not come from the soil. It comes from the air. The carbon in the wood was once carbon dioxide drifting past the leaves. A huge odum tree is, in a sense, made of captured air and water.

## Chlorophyll: the green trap

@image https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/be/Lime_chloroplasts.jpg/960px-Lime_chloroplasts.jpg | Chloroplasts (the green discs) inside leaf cells, seen under a microscope | Mjolk1 · CC0 | https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lime_chloroplasts.jpg

Why are leaves green? Because they contain **chlorophyll**, a pigment inside the **chloroplasts** of the leaf cells.

Chlorophyll's job is to **absorb light energy**. It absorbs red and blue light strongly but reflects green light, which is why we see leaves as green. (If plants absorbed all colours, they would look black.)

The energy absorbed is used to split water and to drive the building of glucose. No chlorophyll, no trapping of energy, no photosynthesis.

Facts to remember:

- Chloroplasts are found mostly in the cells of the **upper half** of the leaf, where light is strongest.
- Each cell may contain dozens of chloroplasts.
- Plants with red or purple leaves still have chlorophyll; other pigments mask the green.
- In the dry season many trees lose their leaves; the chlorophyll breaks down first, which is why leaves turn yellow before they fall.

Chlorophyll is to a leaf what a solar panel is to a house: the part that catches the light.
===
## The leaf: a factory built for the job

A leaf is shaped by its work. Look at any leaf and you will see:

**Broad and flat** – a large surface to catch as much light as possible.

**Thin** – light reaches all the cells, and gases do not have far to travel.

**Veins** – tubes that bring water (xylem) and carry away the glucose made (phloem).

**Stomata** – pores, mostly underneath, that let carbon dioxide in and oxygen and water vapour out. Each is opened and closed by two **guard cells**.

**A waxy layer (cuticle)** on top – stops the leaf drying out.

**Palisade cells** – tall cells packed with chloroplasts just under the upper surface, where most photosynthesis happens.

**Spongy cells with air spaces** – below the palisade layer, letting gases move freely.

## Stomata: the leaf's doors

@image https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8e/Stomata_of_Tradescantia_spathacea_leaves.jpg/960px-Stomata_of_Tradescantia_spathacea_leaves.jpg | Stomata on the surface of a leaf, each with two guard cells, under the microscope | Trương Minh Khải · CC BY 4.0 | https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Stomata_of_Tradescantia_spathacea_leaves.jpg

**Stomata** are tiny pores on the surface of leaves. A single leaf may have hundreds of thousands. Through them:

- **carbon dioxide** comes in for photosynthesis
- **oxygen** goes out (made by photosynthesis)
- **water vapour** goes out (transpiration)

Each stoma is bordered by two curved **guard cells**. When the guard cells are full of water they bow apart and the pore opens; when they lose water they go limp and the pore closes.

Stomata are usually **open in the day** (when light is available for photosynthesis) and **closed at night**. They also close in very hot, dry weather to save water, even in daylight; that is why plants slow down in the heat of the afternoon.

Most stomata are on the **underside** of the leaf. It is cooler there and less water is lost.

| Needed | Where from | How it gets in |
|---|---|---|
| Light | the Sun | absorbed by chlorophyll |
| Water | soil | roots, xylem, veins |
| Carbon dioxide | air | stomata |
| Chlorophyll | made by the plant | inside chloroplasts |

The leaf is built for the job: broad, thin, veined, with stomata underneath and palisade cells on top.

## Testing a leaf for starch

@video 0s_xZqvwm_s | Use iodine to test a leaf for starch | Plant Physiology | Biology | KClassScienceChannel

How do we **know** a leaf has been photosynthesising? Glucose is quickly turned into **starch** and stored in the leaf. Starch turns **iodine solution** from yellow-brown to **blue-black**. So if a leaf contains starch, it has been making food.

**The method** (your teacher will handle the hot water and ethanol):

1. **Boil the leaf in water** for one minute. This kills it and softens it.
2. **Put it in a tube of ethanol** standing in the hot water (not over a flame; ethanol catches fire). The ethanol removes the chlorophyll. The leaf turns pale; the ethanol turns green.
3. **Dip the leaf in warm water** to soften it again.
4. **Spread it on a white tile** and add a few drops of **iodine solution**.
5. **Blue-black** = starch present. **Yellow-brown** = no starch.

Removing the chlorophyll in step 2 matters: without it, the green would hide the colour change.
===
## Fair tests: light and starch

@video uiwlHJ1Dm6Q | Testing a Leaf for Starch | Science4Breakfast

To show that **light** is needed for photosynthesis:

1. **Destarch** a potted plant by leaving it in a dark cupboard for 48 hours. It uses up its stored starch, so we start from zero.
2. Cover part of one leaf with a strip of **black paper** or foil on both sides, held with paper clips.
3. Put the plant in bright light for a day.
4. Remove the leaf, take off the paper and test it for starch.

**Result:** the parts that were in the light turn **blue-black**; the covered strip stays **yellow-brown**.

**Conclusion:** starch (and so glucose) was only made where light reached. Light is needed for photosynthesis.

This is a **fair test** because everything about the two parts of the leaf is the same (same leaf, same water, same air, same chlorophyll) except the one thing we changed: **light**.
===
## Fair tests: chlorophyll and starch

@image https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e7/Variegated_Leaf_with_Prominent_Venation.jpg/960px-Variegated_Leaf_with_Prominent_Venation.jpg | A variegated leaf: the white parts contain no chlorophyll | Newagedawning · CC BY 4.0 | https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Variegated_Leaf_with_Prominent_Venation.jpg

Some plants have **variegated** leaves: part green, part white or cream. The white parts have **no chlorophyll**. They are perfect for a test.

1. Destarch the plant for 48 hours.
2. Place it in bright light for a day.
3. Draw a careful picture of one variegated leaf, marking which parts are green and which are white.
4. Remove the leaf and test it for starch.

**Result:** only the parts that were **green** turn blue-black. The white parts stay yellow-brown, even though they received the same light, water and carbon dioxide.

**Conclusion:** chlorophyll is needed for photosynthesis.

## Fair tests: carbon dioxide and starch

To show that **carbon dioxide** is needed, we must take it away from one plant and not the other.

1. Destarch two similar potted plants.
2. Cover each with a clear plastic bag, sealed at the pot.
3. In bag **A**, put a small dish of **soda lime** or sodium hydroxide, which absorbs carbon dioxide from the air inside.
4. In bag **B**, put a dish of **water** (no effect), so that both bags are set up the same way.
5. Leave both in bright light for a day, then test a leaf from each.

**Result:** the leaf from plant **B** turns blue-black; the leaf from plant **A** stays yellow-brown.

**Conclusion:** without carbon dioxide, no starch is made. Carbon dioxide is needed for photosynthesis.

Plant B is the **control**: it shows that it was the missing carbon dioxide, not the bag or the dish, that stopped photosynthesis. Every good experiment has one.
===
## Showing oxygen is given off

Water plants make this easy. A pond weed such as *Elodea* or *Hydrilla*, which you can find in slow streams and ponds, gives off bubbles when it photosynthesises.

1. Put a sprig of pond weed in a beaker of water.
2. Cover it with an upside-down **funnel**, and over the funnel's stem place a **test tube** full of water.
3. Put the beaker in bright light.

Bubbles rise from the cut stem and collect in the test tube, pushing out the water. After a day or two there is enough gas to test.

4. Lift the tube out with your thumb over the end, and put a **glowing splint** into it. The splint **relights**.

Only **oxygen** relights a glowing splint. So the gas the plant gave off is oxygen.

You can also use this set-up to **compare rates**: count bubbles per minute in bright light, then in dim light. More light, more bubbles, faster photosynthesis.
===

## What is respiration?

Now the second half of the story. Plants have made glucose. How does a cell, in a plant or in you, get the **energy** out of it?

**Respiration** is the process by which living cells break down glucose, using oxygen, to release energy. Carbon dioxide and water are produced.

Do not confuse it with **breathing**. Breathing is the movement of air in and out of the lungs. Respiration is a **chemical reaction inside every cell**: in your muscles, your brain, your skin; in a leaf, a root, a seed; in a fish, a fly, a mushroom and a bacterium.

It never stops. Even when you are asleep, every cell is respiring to keep you alive. A plant respires day and night, even while it photosynthesises.

The energy released is used for all the things living things do: movement, growth, warmth, repair, making new substances.

Respiration is, in a way, a slow and controlled kind of burning: glucose is the fuel, oxygen is needed, and energy and carbon dioxide are given out. But it happens at body temperature, in small steps, without any flame.
===
## The word equation for respiration

@video eJ9Zjc-jdys | Cellular Respiration (UPDATED) | Amoeba Sisters

> **glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water (+ energy released)**

Compare it with photosynthesis:

> carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (light energy taken in)

They are **the same equation backwards**. Photosynthesis builds glucose and stores energy; respiration breaks glucose and releases it.

We write energy in brackets because energy is not a substance; it is not a product you could pour out. It is released as the bonds in glucose are broken and new ones formed in carbon dioxide and water.

The kind of respiration described here needs oxygen and is called **aerobic** respiration. (There is also **anaerobic** respiration, without oxygen, which releases much less energy; you will meet it in Basic 9. It is what makes your legs ache in a sprint, and what yeast does when it makes bread rise or palm wine ferment.)

## Where respiration happens

Respiration happens inside **cells**, in tiny structures called **mitochondria** (one is a **mitochondrion**). You could think of them as the cell's power stations.

Cells that need a lot of energy have many mitochondria:

- **muscle cells**, which contract constantly
- **liver cells**, which carry out hundreds of chemical jobs
- **sperm cells**, which must swim
- the growing tip of a plant **root**

Cells that need less energy have fewer.

The glucose reaches the cells through the **blood** (in animals) or the **phloem** (in plants). The oxygen reaches animal cells from the lungs or gills via the blood; in plants it enters through stomata and the spaces between cells, and in roots from air in the soil.

That last point explains something farmers know: a field that stays **waterlogged** kills crops. Water fills the air spaces in the soil, the roots cannot get oxygen, the root cells cannot respire, and the plant dies from the bottom up.
===
## Evidence for respiration

How do we know respiration is happening?

**Carbon dioxide is given out.** Breathe out through a straw into **limewater**. It turns **milky**. Limewater is the test for carbon dioxide. Germinating seeds in a sealed jar also turn limewater milky; the seeds are respiring.

**Heat is given out.** Put germinating beans in a vacuum flask with a thermometer; put boiled (dead) beans in another flask as a control. After a day, the live beans' flask is warmer. Energy released by respiration has escaped as heat. (A heap of damp grass or cocoa husks warming up as it rots is the same thing: microbes respiring.)

**Oxygen is used up.** Candle flames in a sealed jar with germinating seeds go out sooner than in a jar without seeds.

**Mass is lost.** A seed loses mass as it germinates in the dark, because the glucose in its stores is being broken down to carbon dioxide and water, which leave.

## Recap: respiration

**Respiration** releases energy from glucose in every living cell, all the time.

> glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water (+ energy)

- It is **not** breathing; breathing just brings oxygen to the blood.
- It happens in **mitochondria**.
- The energy is used for movement, growth, warmth, repair and making new substances.
- Evidence: carbon dioxide turns limewater milky; germinating seeds get warm, lose mass and use up oxygen.

## Two processes, one cycle

@image https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7d/Looking_up_into_a_dense_green_forest_canopy.jpg/960px-Looking_up_into_a_dense_green_forest_canopy.jpg | A forest canopy: by day its leaves make far more oxygen than the trees use | Krishna k. sahh · CC0 | https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Looking_up_into_a_dense_green_forest_canopy.jpg

Put the two equations together and a beautiful pattern appears.

**Photosynthesis** uses carbon dioxide and water, makes glucose and oxygen, and **stores** energy from the Sun.

**Respiration** uses glucose and oxygen, makes carbon dioxide and water, and **releases** that energy.

What one process makes, the other needs. The oxygen you breathe was made by a plant. The carbon dioxide you breathe out will be taken into a leaf. The glucose in your waakye was built in a rice plant from carbon dioxide you, or a goat, or a fire, once released.

This is the **carbon and oxygen cycle**. Carbon atoms circle for ever between air and living things, with energy from the Sun driving the loop.

Without photosynthesis, oxygen would run out and food would run out. Without respiration, no living thing could use the food. Both matter, always.
===
## Do plants respire too?

Yes, **always**. A common mistake is to say that plants photosynthesise and animals respire. In fact:

- **Animals** respire only.
- **Plants** respire **and** photosynthesise.

In the **day**, a plant does both. Photosynthesis is usually much faster, so overall the plant **takes in** carbon dioxide and **gives out** oxygen. The carbon dioxide its own respiration makes is used straight away for photosynthesis.

At **night**, there is no light, so photosynthesis stops. The plant still respires: it **takes in** oxygen and **gives out** carbon dioxide, just like you.

Over a whole day, a healthy plant makes much more glucose than it breaks down. The extra is what becomes new leaves, roots, fruit and stored starch: the plant's growth, and our food.

## Why both matter: the balance of gases

The air has stayed roughly **21 % oxygen** for a very long time. That is no accident. Photosynthesis adds oxygen; respiration and burning remove it. The two have been roughly in balance.

Carbon dioxide, however, is rising. Burning **fossil fuels** (petrol, diesel, coal, gas) releases carbon that was locked away for millions of years, far faster than plants can take it up. Cutting down forests removes the very leaves that would absorb it.

Carbon dioxide traps heat, so the Earth is warming. In Ghana this shows up as hotter dry seasons, less predictable rains and coastal erosion.

What helps:

- **Planting trees** and protecting forests such as Kakum and the Atewa Range
- **Using less fuel**: walking, sharing transport, efficient stoves
- **Clean energy**: solar panels on roofs do, in a way, what leaves do: catch sunlight

Understanding these two equations is the first step to understanding one of the biggest problems of our time.

## Food chains start with a leaf

@image https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/98/Vegetables_Market_in_Northern_Ghana.jpg | A vegetable market in northern Ghana: every item here started as glucose made in a leaf | Ibn Shiraz · CC BY-SA 4.0 | https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Vegetables_Market_in_Northern_Ghana.jpg

A **food chain** shows who eats whom. Every food chain begins with a **producer**: a plant, or a tiny floating alga in the sea or a lake, which makes its food by photosynthesis.

> grass → grasshopper → lizard → hawk
> maize → goat → human
> algae → tilapia → human

Everything after the producer is a **consumer**. It gets glucose by eating. At every step, each creature respires and uses most of the energy it took in for moving and keeping warm, so only a small part passes to the next eater. That is why there are many grasshoppers but few hawks.

The energy in every chain was once **sunlight**, trapped by chlorophyll. Even the energy you used to turn this page came, by a long road, from the Sun.

## Photosynthesis and farming

Farmers are photosynthesis managers, whether or not they use the word. Everything they do increases how much glucose their crops can make and store.

**Spacing** – plants too close shade one another; too far apart wastes land. Maize is often planted about 75 cm between rows.

**Weeding** – weeds steal light, water and minerals.

**Watering** – without water, stomata close and photosynthesis stops.

**Fertiliser and manure** – add minerals such as **nitrates** so the plant can turn glucose into proteins for growth.

**Timing** – planting at the start of the rains gives seedlings water when they need it most.

**Shade for cocoa** – cocoa is a forest plant; young cocoa grows better under a light shade of plantain or taller trees, which cut harsh midday sun and keep moisture in.

**Greenhouses** – in some vegetable farms near Accra and Kumasi, plastic houses keep water in and allow year-round growing.

## What the plant does with glucose

Glucose is sweet, soluble and quickly made. A plant does five things with it.

**1. Respires it** – some glucose is broken down at once to release energy for the plant's own work.

**2. Stores it as starch** – starch is insoluble and compact, ideal for storing in leaves by day, and for the long term in roots (cassava, yam), tubers (potato), stems (sugar cane stores sugar itself) and seeds (maize, rice, beans).

**3. Builds cellulose** – thousands of glucose units joined into tough fibres make the **cell wall** of every plant cell. Wood, cotton and paper are mostly cellulose.

**4. Makes proteins** – glucose plus **nitrates** from the soil gives the amino acids that build proteins for growth and enzymes.

**5. Makes oils and fats** – stored in seeds such as groundnut, palm kernel and shea.

When you eat gari, chew sugar cane, wear a cotton shirt or cook with palm oil, you are using glucose that a leaf made from air, water and sunlight.
===
## Minerals from the soil

Photosynthesis needs only carbon dioxide and water, but a plant is more than glucose. To build proteins, chlorophyll and new cells it needs **minerals**, dissolved in soil water and taken in by the roots.

| Mineral | Needed for | If it is short |
|---|---|---|
| **Nitrates** | making proteins for growth | small plant, yellow older leaves |
| **Magnesium** | making chlorophyll | yellow leaves, poor photosynthesis |
| **Phosphates** | roots, energy transfer | poor roots, purple-tinged leaves |
| **Potassium** | healthy leaves and flowers | brown edges on leaves |

Farmers add these as **fertiliser** (bags marked NPK: nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium) or as **manure and compost**, which release minerals slowly as they rot.

Growing **beans and groundnuts** also adds nitrates: bacteria in lumps on their roots turn nitrogen gas from the air into nitrates. That is why farmers rotate maize with legumes.

## Life in the water

Photosynthesis is not only for land plants. In the sea, lakes and the Volta, tiny floating **algae** (phytoplankton) and larger seaweeds photosynthesise just as leaves do: they contain chlorophyll, take in carbon dioxide dissolved in the water, and give off oxygen.

The numbers are enormous. Algae in the oceans make **about half** of all the oxygen produced on Earth. Every second breath you take comes from the sea.

They are also the **producers** at the bottom of every water food chain:

> algae → tiny animals → small fish → tilapia or tuna → people

Fishermen at Elmina, Tema and on Lake Volta depend on this invisible photosynthesis.

Algae need light, so they live near the **surface**. Muddy water from erosion, or water choked with weeds, blocks the light, so fewer algae grow and fish become scarce. Protecting rivers from silt and pollution protects the whole chain from the first leaf-like cell to the last plate of fish.
===
## Seeds: packed lunches

A seed is a baby plant with a **food store**, wrapped in a coat. The store is starch, protein or oil made by the parent plant from the glucose of photosynthesis.

When a seed **germinates**:

1. It takes in water and swells.
2. Enzymes turn the stored starch back into glucose.
3. The cells **respire** that glucose to release energy for growth.
4. A root grows down and a shoot grows up.

All this happens in the **dark**, underground, with **no photosynthesis**. The seedling lives entirely on its packed lunch until the first green leaves open above the soil. Then it starts to make its own food, and the store is no longer needed.

This explains two things:

- A germinating seed **loses mass** and **gives out carbon dioxide**, because it respires but does not photosynthesise.
- A seed planted **too deep** uses up its store before the shoot reaches the light, and dies.

### Try this

Soak some beans overnight, wrap them in damp paper and keep them in the dark. Record what happens each day for a week.
===
## How fast? Limiting factors

Photosynthesis does not always run at full speed. Its **rate** depends on light, carbon dioxide and temperature. Whichever of these is **in shortest supply** sets the speed. It is called the **limiting factor**.

Think of a chop bar with three cooks: one for rice, one for stew, one for serving. If the stew cook is slow, it does not matter how fast the others work; plates leave at the stew cook's pace.

- **Early morning:** light is dim, so **light** limits the rate. As the sun rises, photosynthesis speeds up.
- **Bright midday:** light is plentiful, so now **carbon dioxide** (only 0.04 % of air) limits the rate.
- **Cool harmattan dawn:** low **temperature** slows the enzymes, so the rate is low even in sunshine.

If the limiting factor is increased, the rate rises until something else becomes limiting.

### Try this

In the pond-weed experiment, moving the lamp closer increases bubbles up to a point, after which they stop increasing. Which factor has become limiting, and how could you test your answer?
===
## Breathing, exercise and respiration

When you run, your muscles respire faster to release more energy. They need more glucose and more oxygen, and they make more carbon dioxide.

Your body responds:

- **Breathing** becomes faster and deeper, to take in oxygen and remove carbon dioxide.
- Your **heart** beats faster, to carry glucose and oxygen to the muscles quickly.
- You feel **warm** and sweat, because respiration releases energy partly as heat.

If you sprint flat out, the oxygen cannot arrive fast enough. Muscles switch to **anaerobic** respiration for a short time, producing **lactic acid**, which is what makes your legs burn and ache. After the race you keep panting to pay back the oxygen debt.

### Try this

Count your breaths for one minute sitting still. Then do 30 star jumps and count again. Record both. Explain the difference using the word *respiration*.

## Recap: putting it together

| | Photosynthesis | Respiration |
|---|---|---|
| Where | chloroplasts, mainly leaves | mitochondria, all cells |
| When | daylight only | all the time |
| Who | green plants, algae | all living things |
| Takes in | carbon dioxide, water | glucose, oxygen |
| Gives out | glucose, oxygen | carbon dioxide, water |
| Energy | stored (from light) | released |

The two processes are opposites, and together they form the carbon and oxygen cycle that keeps the air breathable and every food chain fed.

## Experiment design: your own fair test

You now know enough to design an investigation. Suppose you want to find out whether **the colour of light** affects how fast pond weed photosynthesises.

**Question:** Does pond weed make more bubbles in red, green or blue light?

**Variables:**

- **Independent** (what I change): colour of the light (use coloured cellophane over a lamp).
- **Dependent** (what I measure): bubbles per minute.
- **Controlled** (kept the same): same sprig of weed, same lamp, same distance, same water, same temperature, same time of counting.

**Method:** set up the pond weed as before; cover the lamp with red cellophane; wait two minutes; count bubbles for one minute; repeat three times and average. Repeat for green and blue.

**Prediction:** green light will give the fewest bubbles, because chlorophyll reflects green.

Repeating and averaging makes the result more **reliable**. Changing only one thing makes it a **fair test**.
===
## Reading results

Here are results from the coloured-light experiment.

| Light colour | Bubbles per minute (3 trials) | Average |
|---|---|---|
| Red | 24, 22, 26 | 24 |
| Green | 6, 8, 7 | 7 |
| Blue | 20, 23, 20 | 21 |

**What the results show:** red and blue light gave many bubbles; green gave few. The prediction was right: chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light well and reflects green, so green light is of little use for photosynthesis.

**Evaluating:**

- Bubble size varied; a better method would collect the gas and measure its volume.
- The lamp also warms the water a little; a glass of water between lamp and weed would absorb the heat.
- Three trials is a reasonable start; five would be better.

Being able to say what was good and what could be improved is part of being a scientist, and it earns marks in examinations.

## Common mistakes

**"Plants breathe in carbon dioxide and breathe out oxygen."**
Plants do not breathe. They **take in** carbon dioxide through stomata by diffusion, and they also respire, taking in oxygen, all the time.

**"Plants get their food from the soil."**
They get **water and minerals** from the soil. Their **food** (glucose) is made in the leaves from carbon dioxide and water.

**"Respiration is breathing."**
Breathing moves air. Respiration is a chemical reaction in cells.

**"Only animals respire."**
Every living cell respires, including every plant cell, day and night.

**"Chlorophyll is a reactant."**
It is not used up. It is a pigment that traps light. Write it on the arrow, not on the left.

**"The iodine turned blue, so the leaf has glucose."**
Iodine tests for **starch**, which the plant makes from glucose.

**"Photosynthesis gives the plant energy."**
Photosynthesis **stores** energy in glucose. **Respiration** releases it.

## Glossary

**Photosynthesis** — the process by which green plants make glucose from carbon dioxide and water using light energy.

**Respiration** — the release of energy from glucose in living cells; aerobic respiration uses oxygen.

**Chlorophyll** — the green pigment that absorbs light energy.

**Chloroplast** — the structure in a plant cell where photosynthesis happens.

**Mitochondrion** — the structure in a cell where respiration happens.

**Glucose** — a simple sugar; the plant's food and the fuel for respiration.

**Starch** — a storage substance made from glucose; turns iodine blue-black.

**Stomata** — pores in the leaf, each between two guard cells, through which gases pass.

**Transpiration** — loss of water vapour from leaves.

**Xylem / phloem** — tubes carrying water up / glucose around the plant.

**Producer** — an organism that makes its own food; the start of every food chain.

**Fair test** — an experiment in which only one thing is changed.

**Control** — the part of an experiment that shows what happens when the one thing is not changed.
