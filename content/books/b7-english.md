## Two ways to tell what someone said

Every day you report what other people have said. "Mum said we should come home early." "Kofi told me the test is on Friday." There are two ways to do this in English.

**Direct speech** gives the speaker's exact words, inside quotation marks:

Ama said, "I am hungry."

**Indirect speech** (also called **reported speech**) gives the meaning of the words, without quotation marks:

Ama said that she was hungry.

Both sentences tell us the same thing. But look closely. In the second one, "I" has become "she", and "am" has become "was". The words have changed because the person telling the story is not Ama, and the moment of speaking has passed.

This book will teach you how to:

- punctuate direct speech correctly
- choose good reporting verbs
- change direct speech into indirect speech, and back again
- write lively dialogue in your own stories

Let us begin with the exact words.
===
## Direct speech: the exact words

Direct speech repeats exactly what a person said, word for word. We mark it off with **quotation marks**, which Ghanaian teachers often call **inverted commas** or **speech marks**.

@image https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7f/Speech_bubble.svg/960px-Speech_bubble.svg.png | Think of quotation marks as a speech bubble drawn in writing | Amada44 · Public domain | https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Speech_bubble.svg

Look at these examples:

- "Pass me the pencil," said Abena.
- Kwame shouted, "Goal!"
- "Where is the chalk?" asked the teacher.

In each one, the words inside the quotation marks are the speaker's own. Nothing has been changed. If Kwame shouted "Goal!", we write "Goal!" and not "a goal".

Direct speech is powerful in stories because it lets the reader hear the characters. You can show how someone is feeling by the words they choose, not just by describing them.

**Try this**
Listen for one minute at break time. Write down three things you hear people say, exactly as they said them, inside quotation marks.
===
## Quotation marks: the basics

Quotation marks come in pairs. One opens the speech and one closes it. In British English we usually use single marks (' ') in printed books, but double marks (" ") are also correct and are common in schools. Choose one style and keep to it.

**Rule 1.** Put the speaker's exact words inside the marks.

"We are going to the market."

**Rule 2.** Start the speech with a capital letter, even if it comes in the middle of a sentence.

Ama said, "We are going to the market."

**Rule 3.** The punctuation that belongs to the speech goes **inside** the closing mark.

"We are going to the market," said Ama.
"Are we going to the market?" asked Kofi.
"Hurry up!" shouted Abena.

Notice that when the sentence continues after the speech (as in "said Ama"), the full stop inside the speech becomes a **comma**. The question mark and exclamation mark stay as they are.

**Try this**
Add quotation marks: I have finished my homework said Kwame.
===
## Speech first or speaker first?

There are two main patterns for a line of direct speech.

**Pattern A: speaker first**

Kofi said, "The bus is late."

- Write the speaker and the reporting verb.
- Add a comma.
- Open the quotation marks.
- Write the speech with a capital letter and end it with a full stop, question mark or exclamation mark.
- Close the quotation marks.

**Pattern B: speech first**

"The bus is late," said Kofi.

- Open the quotation marks and write the speech with a capital letter.
- End the speech with a comma (if it would have been a full stop), a question mark or an exclamation mark.
- Close the quotation marks.
- Write the speaker and the verb, then a full stop.

Both patterns are correct. Good writers mix them so the writing does not sound the same every time.

**Try this**
Rewrite each sentence in the other pattern:
1. Abena said, "I left my book at home."
2. "Can we start now?" asked the teacher.
===
## Speaker in the middle

A third pattern puts the speaker in the middle of the speech. This is very common in storybooks.

"If we leave now," said Ama, "we will reach Kumasi before dark."

Here the speech is split into two parts. Look carefully at the punctuation:

- The first part ends with a comma inside the quotation marks.
- Then comes the speaker and the verb, followed by another comma.
- The second part opens with new quotation marks and a **small letter**, because it continues the same sentence.

If the first part is a complete sentence, the second part starts with a capital letter and the speaker is followed by a full stop:

"We are late," said Ama. "The bus has already left."

**Try this**
Punctuate these:
1. I think said Kofi that the answer is seven
2. Stop said the teacher you are making too much noise
===
## Each new speaker, a new line

When two or more people are talking, start a **new paragraph** every time the speaker changes. This is the most important rule for writing dialogue. It lets the reader follow who is speaking without being told every time.

"Have you seen my football?" asked Kwame.

"No," said Abena. "Did you leave it at school?"

"I don't think so. I had it on the trotro."

"Then ask the driver tomorrow."

Notice two things. First, after the first two lines, the writer stops saying "said Kwame" and "said Abena". We know who is talking because the speakers take turns. Second, each new line of speech begins at the start of a new line.

Write a four-line conversation between two friends about what to buy at the canteen. Use a new line for each speaker.

## Reporting verbs

The word that tells us someone spoke is the **reporting verb**. "Said" is the most common, and it is a perfectly good word. But English has many others, and each one adds information.

**Loud or soft:** shouted, yelled, screamed, whispered, murmured, muttered

**Asking:** asked, enquired, wondered, demanded

**Answering:** replied, answered, responded

**Feeling:** laughed, cried, sobbed, snapped, sighed, groaned

**Telling:** explained, announced, warned, promised, admitted, agreed, complained, begged, insisted

Compare these two sentences:

"Give me back my pen," said Ama.
"Give me back my pen," snapped Ama.

The second one tells us Ama is angry, without using the word "angry".

Be careful not to overdo it. A page full of "exclaimed" and "ejaculated" sounds silly. Use "said" most of the time, and save the stronger verbs for moments that need them.

**Try this**
Choose a reporting verb for each: (a) a pupil who has lost her money, (b) a referee, (c) someone telling a secret.
===
## Recap: direct speech

Before we move on to indirect speech, check that you have these points.

- **Direct speech** gives the exact words, inside quotation marks.
- Start the speech with a **capital letter**.
- Punctuation belonging to the speech goes **inside** the closing mark.
- When the speaker comes after the speech, a full stop becomes a **comma**: "I am tired," said Kofi.
- Question marks and exclamation marks stay as they are: "Are you tired?" asked Kofi.
- Three patterns: speaker first, speech first, speaker in the middle.
- **New speaker, new line.**
- The **reporting verb** (said, asked, shouted…) tells us how the words were spoken.

**Quick check**
Correct the mistakes in these:
1. "where is the market" asked Ama.
2. Kofi said "I will come tomorrow".
3. "Run" shouted the coach, "The bus is leaving!"

There are at least five mistakes altogether. Find them all.

## Indirect speech: reporting the meaning

Now we come to the second way of telling what someone said. **Indirect speech** reports the meaning of the words, not the exact words. There are no quotation marks.

Direct: Kofi said, "I am tired."
Indirect: Kofi said that he was tired.

Direct: "We will visit Grandma on Sunday," said Ama.
Indirect: Ama said that they would visit Grandma on Sunday.

Three things changed when we moved from direct to indirect:

1. The quotation marks disappeared.
2. The word **that** joined the two parts (it can often be left out, but it helps at first).
3. Some words changed: "I" became "he", "am" became "was", "we" became "they", "will" became "would".

Why do the words change? Because the person reporting is a different person, speaking at a later time. Kofi said "I", but when Ama reports it, she is talking about Kofi, so she says "he".

The next pages show each change in detail.

## Changing the pronouns

**Pronouns** are the little words that stand for people: I, you, he, she, we, they, me, us, my, your and so on. In indirect speech they usually change, because the reporter is not the original speaker.

The rule is simple: **think about who is really being talked about.**

Direct: Abena said, "I have finished **my** work."
Indirect: Abena said that **she** had finished **her** work.

Abena was talking about herself. When we report it, we are talking about Abena, so "I" becomes "she" and "my" becomes "her".

Direct: Kofi said to Ama, "**You** are late."
Indirect: Kofi told Ama that **she** was late.

Kofi was talking to Ama. We are talking about Ama, so "you" becomes "she".

Direct: "**We** will win," said the players.
Indirect: The players said that **they** would win.

**Try this**
Change the pronouns:
1. Kwame said, "I have lost my pen."
2. The teacher said to us, "You must finish your work."
3. "We are going home," said the girls.
===
## Changing the tense: one step back

When we report speech, the moment of speaking has usually passed. So the verb usually moves **one step back** into the past. This is called **backshift**.

Here is the pattern:

- Present simple → past simple
  "I **like** mangoes." → She said that she **liked** mangoes.
- Present continuous → past continuous
  "I **am reading**." → He said that he **was reading**.
- Present perfect → past perfect
  "I **have eaten**." → She said that she **had eaten**.
- Past simple → past perfect
  "I **saw** the match." → He said that he **had seen** the match.
- will → would
  "I **will** help." → She said that she **would** help.
- can → could
  "I **can** swim." → He said that he **could** swim.
- must → had to
  "You **must** go." → She said that I **had to** go.

**Try this**
Backshift each verb:
1. "I am tired."
2. "We have finished."
3. "She can sing."
4. "They will arrive at six."
===
## Worked example: a message for Mum

Let us change a whole piece of direct speech, step by step.

Ama's teacher said to her, "You have done very well this term. I will give you a prize on Friday."

Ama goes home and tells her mother. She cannot use the exact words, because she is reporting.

**Step 1: Remove the quotation marks and add "that".**
The teacher told me that…

**Step 2: Change the pronouns.** "You" (meaning Ama) becomes "I". "I" (meaning the teacher) becomes "she".
…that I have done very well this term. She will give me a prize on Friday.

**Step 3: Backshift the verbs.** "have done" → "had done". "will give" → "would give".

**Finished:**
The teacher told me that I had done very well this term and that she would give me a prize on Friday.

Read the direct and indirect versions aloud. Both carry the same news, but the second one sounds like Ama telling the story.

**Try this**
Report this to a friend: Kofi said to you, "I can lend you my book. You can return it tomorrow."

## Words for time and place

Because the reporter is in a different time and place, some other words change too.

| Direct speech | Indirect speech |
|---|---|
| now | then |
| today | that day |
| tonight | that night |
| tomorrow | the next day / the following day |
| yesterday | the day before / the previous day |
| next week | the following week |
| last week | the week before |
| here | there |
| this | that |
| these | those |

Direct: "I will see you **here tomorrow**," said Kwame.
Indirect: Kwame said that he would see me **there the next day**.

Direct: "I bought **these** yams **yesterday**," said the trader.
Indirect: The trader said that she had bought **those** yams **the day before**.

These changes only make sense if the time really has moved. If Kwame spoke this morning and you report it this afternoon, "tomorrow" is still tomorrow, and you need not change it. Think about what is true, not just about the rule.
===
## Reporting questions

Questions change in a special way. In indirect speech, a question is **no longer a question**. The question mark goes, and the word order becomes the order of an ordinary statement.

**Yes/no questions** use "if" or "whether":

Direct: "Are you coming?" asked Ama.
Indirect: Ama asked **if** I was coming.

Direct: "Have you seen Kofi?" she asked.
Indirect: She asked **whether** I had seen Kofi.

**Wh- questions** keep the question word (what, where, when, why, who, how):

Direct: "Where do you live?" asked the nurse.
Indirect: The nurse asked **where I lived**.

Direct: "What time is it?" asked Kwame.
Indirect: Kwame asked **what time it was**.

Look at the word order. "Where do you live?" becomes "where I lived", not "where did I live". The helping verb "do" disappears and the subject comes before the verb, as in a statement.

**Try this**
1. "Why are you late?" asked the teacher.
2. "Do you like fufu?" asked Abena.
3. "How much is the kenkey?" asked Mum.
===
## Reporting commands and requests

Commands and requests use **"to" + verb** in indirect speech. The reporting verb is usually "told", "ordered", "asked" or "begged".

**Commands**

Direct: "Sit down," said the teacher.
Indirect: The teacher **told** us **to sit** down.

Direct: "Close the gate!" shouted Dad.
Indirect: Dad **ordered** me **to close** the gate.

**Negative commands** use "not to":

Direct: "Don't touch the stove," said Mum.
Indirect: Mum told me **not to touch** the stove.

**Requests** sound softer, so we use "asked" or "begged":

Direct: "Please help me carry this," said Ama.
Indirect: Ama **asked** me **to help** her carry it.

Direct: "Please don't tell anyone," whispered Kofi.
Indirect: Kofi **begged** me **not to tell** anyone.

Notice that "please" disappears. Its politeness is carried by the verb "asked" or "begged" instead.

**Try this**
1. "Open your books," said the teacher.
2. "Don't run in the corridor!" shouted the prefect.
3. "Please lend me five cedis," said Kwame.
===
## Recap: direct to indirect

Here is the full checklist for turning direct speech into indirect speech.

1. **Remove the quotation marks.**
2. **Add a joining word:** "that" for statements, "if/whether" for yes/no questions, the question word for wh- questions, "to" for commands.
3. **Change the pronouns** so they point at the right people.
4. **Backshift the verb** one step into the past (am → was, have → had, will → would, can → could).
5. **Change time and place words** if the time really has moved (today → that day, here → there).
6. For **questions**, use statement word order and drop the question mark.
7. For **commands**, use "to" + verb, or "not to" + verb.

**Quick check**
Report each of these. Kofi is speaking to you.
1. "I am waiting for my brother."
2. "Have you finished the test?"
3. "Where did you buy that bag?"
4. "Give me the ball."
5. "Don't forget your homework tomorrow."

## When the tense does not change

Backshift is the usual rule, but it has exceptions. The tense can stay the same when what was said is **still true now**, or when the reporting verb is in the **present**.

**Still true**

Direct: "Accra is the capital of Ghana," said the teacher.
Indirect: The teacher said that Accra **is** the capital of Ghana.

Accra is still the capital, so "is" can stay. (Saying "was the capital" is also correct, but it might make a reader wonder if it has changed.)

**Reporting verb in the present**

Direct: Kofi says, "I am hungry."
Indirect: Kofi says that he **is** hungry.

Because "says" is present, Kofi is speaking right now. Nothing has moved into the past, so nothing needs to shift. You will hear this often: "Mum says you have to come home."

**Past perfect does not change**

"I had already eaten" stays as "had already eaten" because there is no tense further back to move to.

**Try this**
Decide whether to backshift:
1. The nurse says, "The clinic opens at eight."
2. Abena said, "Water boils at 100 degrees."
===
## Indirect to direct: going back

You will often be asked to do the reverse: turn indirect speech back into the speaker's exact words. Simply run the checklist backwards.

Indirect: Ama told Kofi that she would meet him there the next day.

**Step 1:** Add quotation marks and remove "that".
**Step 2:** Pronouns: "she" was Ama talking about herself → "I". "Him" was Kofi being spoken to → "you".
**Step 3:** Tense forwards: "would" → "will".
**Step 4:** Time and place: "there" → "here", "the next day" → "tomorrow".

Direct: Ama said to Kofi, "I will meet you here tomorrow."

Another:

Indirect: The teacher asked us whether we had brought our exercise books.
Direct: The teacher asked, "Have you brought your exercise books?"

Remember to bring back the **question mark** and the **question word order**.

**Try this**
Turn these back into direct speech:
1. Kwame said that he had finished his chores.
2. Mum asked me where I had put the keys.
3. The coach told the players not to give up.

## Worked example: a short story in two forms

Here is a scene written in direct speech.

Kwame ran into the house. "Mum, the goat has escaped!" he shouted.

"Which way did it go?" asked Mum.

"Towards the school," said Kwame. "I will go and catch it."

"Take Ama with you," said Mum, "and don't come back without it."

Now here is the same scene in indirect speech.

Kwame ran into the house and shouted to his mother that the goat had escaped. Mum asked which way it had gone. Kwame said that it had gone towards the school and that he would go and catch it. Mum told him to take Ama with him and not to come back without it.

**Compare them.** The direct version is faster and more exciting. We hear the panic in Kwame's voice. The indirect version is calmer and shorter. It feels like someone telling us about it afterwards.

Neither is "better". A good writer chooses the one that suits the moment.

## Why dialogue matters in stories

Dialogue is the parts of a story where characters speak. Stories with good dialogue feel alive. Stories with none can feel like a report.

@image https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/52/Pupil_writing.jpg/960px-Pupil_writing.jpg | A pupil writing. Dialogue is where your characters start to breathe | Amuzujoe · CC BY-SA 4.0 | https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Pupil_writing.jpg

Dialogue does three jobs:

**1. It shows character.** A boy who says "Give it here!" is different from one who says "May I have it, please?"

**2. It moves the story forward.** Characters make plans, argue, reveal secrets and make decisions by talking.

**3. It breaks up long paragraphs.** Short lines of speech give the reader's eyes a rest and make the page look inviting.

Compare:

*Abena was angry that Kofi had broken her ruler.*

"Kofi! That was my only ruler!"

The second one puts us in the room.

**Try this**
Think of a character you know from a story. Write one line of speech that only that character would say.
===
## Making dialogue sound real

Real people do not speak in long, perfect sentences. Good dialogue copies the way people really talk, but tidies it up a little.

**Keep it short.** Most lines of real speech are under ten words.

**Let people interrupt and disagree.**

"I think we should—"
"No. We are going home."

**Use contractions.** "I'm", "don't", "can't" sound more natural than "I am", "do not", "cannot" in speech.

**Let characters not answer the question.**

"Did you take my phone?"
"Why would I want your phone?"

**Use Ghanaian voices when it fits.** Characters can say "chale", "eii" or "oh" the way people do. Just be sure the reader will understand.

**Avoid too much information.** Nobody says, "As you know, my brother Kofi, who is twelve years old, is in Basic 7." Keep information for the narration.

**Try this**
Rewrite this stiff line so it sounds like a real twelve-year-old: "I am unable to attend football practice because I must complete my homework."
===
## Showing feeling without "said angrily"

New writers often add an adverb to every reporting verb: said angrily, said happily, said sadly. It is better to **show** the feeling through the words and the actions.

Weak: "Give me my book," said Ama angrily.

Stronger: Ama snatched at the book. "Give it here."

Stronger still: "Give. Me. My. Book." Ama's hand was shaking.

Three ways to show feeling:

1. **The words themselves.** Short, sharp words show anger. Long, wandering sentences show nervousness.
2. **An action beside the speech.** Ama slammed the door. Kofi looked at his feet. These "action beats" tell us the mood.
3. **A stronger reporting verb.** Snapped, whispered, begged.

You do not need all three at once. One is usually enough.

**Try this**
Write three versions of a character saying "I didn't do it":
- one where they are frightened
- one where they are angry
- one where they are lying
Do not use any adverbs.
===
## Action beats

An **action beat** is a small action placed next to a line of speech, instead of a reporting verb. It shows who is speaking and what they are doing at the same time.

Kofi dropped his bag on the floor. "I'm not going back there."

Here there is no "said". We know Kofi is speaking because the action and the speech are in the same paragraph.

Action beats are useful because:

- they avoid repeating "said" over and over
- they add movement to a scene
- they show feeling without naming it

Punctuation: the action beat is a complete sentence with its own full stop. The speech begins with a capital letter and new quotation marks.

Ama looked at the sky. "It's going to rain."

Not: Ama looked at the sky, "It's going to rain." (The comma is wrong here, because "looked" is not a reporting verb.)

**Try this**
Replace the reporting verb with an action beat:
1. "I'm so tired," said Abena.
2. "Where did you find it?" asked Kwame.
===
## Recap: writing dialogue

You now have the tools for writing speech in your own stories.

- **New speaker, new line.**
- Punctuate carefully: capitals, commas, marks inside the quotation marks.
- Use **said** most of the time. Save strong reporting verbs for strong moments.
- **Show feeling** through words and actions, not adverbs.
- Use **action beats** instead of "said" sometimes.
- Keep lines **short** and natural. Use contractions.
- Let characters **interrupt**, **disagree** and **dodge** questions.
- Mix direct speech with indirect speech. Use indirect speech to summarise and direct speech for the important moments.

**Quick check**
Here is a dull piece of dialogue. Improve it using at least three of the ideas above.

"I am very angry with you," said Kofi angrily. "You lost my ball," he continued angrily. "I am sorry," said Kwame sadly. "I did not mean to lose it," he added.

## Punctuation inside the marks

Here are the details that trip people up. Read them slowly.

**Comma before the speech (speaker first):**
Kofi said, "I'm ready."

**Comma instead of full stop (speech first):**
"I'm ready," said Kofi.
Not: "I'm ready." said Kofi.

**Question and exclamation marks stay:**
"Are you ready?" asked Kofi.
"Ready!" shouted Kofi.
Note that "asked" and "shouted" begin with a small letter even though a question mark or exclamation mark comes before them. They are part of the same sentence.

**A quotation inside speech** uses the other kind of mark:
"Mum said 'no sweets', so I didn't buy any," explained Ama.

**Speech that runs over several paragraphs** opens quotation marks at the start of each paragraph but closes them only at the very end.

**Try this**
Find the one mistake in each line:
1. "Come here". said the teacher.
2. "Where are you going?" Asked Kwame.
3. Ama said "I'll be back soon."

## Common mistakes

**Mistake 1: forgetting the comma.**
Wrong: Kofi said "I am coming."
Right: Kofi said, "I am coming."

**Mistake 2: punctuation outside the marks.**
Wrong: "I am coming", said Kofi.
Right: "I am coming," said Kofi.

**Mistake 3: capital letter after a question mark.**
Wrong: "Are you coming?" Asked Kofi.
Right: "Are you coming?" asked Kofi.

**Mistake 4: keeping the question form in indirect speech.**
Wrong: Kofi asked where was I going.
Right: Kofi asked where I was going.

**Mistake 5: forgetting to change the pronoun.**
Wrong: Kofi said that I was coming. (when Kofi was talking about himself)
Right: Kofi said that he was coming.

**Mistake 6: no new line for a new speaker.**
Wrong: "Hello," said Ama. "Hello," said Kofi.
Right: put each on its own line.

**Mistake 7: too many fancy reporting verbs.**
"Hello," ejaculated Ama. "Hello," retorted Kofi.
Just use "said".

## Reporting verbs with "that"

Some reporting verbs are followed by **that** and a full sentence. These are the everyday verbs for statements.

- say that
- tell someone that
- explain that
- announce that
- admit that
- complain that
- promise that
- agree that
- warn someone that

Examples:

Direct: "I broke the cup," said Kofi.
Indirect: Kofi **admitted that** he had broken the cup.

Direct: "The road is flooded," said the driver.
Indirect: The driver **warned us that** the road was flooded.

Direct: "I will pay you back on Monday," said Ama.
Indirect: Ama **promised that** she would pay me back on Monday.

The reporting verb often replaces a feeling word. Instead of "said sadly that he had broken it", we write "admitted that he had broken it". The verb does the work.

**Say or tell?** We **say** something. We **tell** someone something. "He said that…" but "He told me that…". Never "He said me that".

**Try this**
Report with a good verb: "Okay, you were right," said Kwame.
===
## Reporting verbs with "to"

Other reporting verbs are followed by **to** and a verb. These are for commands, requests, offers and promises.

- tell someone to
- ask someone to
- order someone to
- beg someone to
- warn someone not to
- remind someone to
- advise someone to
- offer to
- promise to
- refuse to
- agree to

Examples:

Direct: "Remember to lock the door," said Mum.
Indirect: Mum **reminded me to lock** the door.

Direct: "I'll carry your bag," said Kofi.
Indirect: Kofi **offered to carry** my bag.

Direct: "I won't eat that," said Abena.
Indirect: Abena **refused to eat** it.

Direct: "You should see a doctor," said Grandma.
Indirect: Grandma **advised me to see** a doctor.

Notice how much shorter the indirect version can be. "I'll carry your bag" becomes a tidy "offered to carry". This is why indirect speech is so useful for summarising.

**Try this**
1. "Please, please come to my party," said Ama.
2. "I'll help you with your maths," said Kwame.
3. "Don't swim in the lagoon," said the fisherman.

## Recap: reporting verbs

Reporting verbs do two jobs: they tell us **that** someone spoke, and they tell us **how** or **why**.

**For direct speech,** choose a verb that fits the voice: said, asked, shouted, whispered, snapped, laughed. Use "said" most of the time.

**For indirect speech,** choose a verb that fits the meaning:

- Statements: **said that, told me that, explained that, admitted that, warned that, promised that**
- Questions: **asked if, asked whether, asked where/when/why/how**
- Commands and requests: **told me to, ordered me to, asked me to, begged me to, reminded me to, warned me not to**
- Offers and refusals: **offered to, refused to, agreed to**

Remember: **say** something, **tell** someone something.

2. "I'll wash the plates."
3. "Yes, it was me who ate the last piece."
4. "I'm not going."
5. "Be careful, the floor is wet."

## Worked example: from a conversation to a report

Kofi overhears this conversation and later tells his sister about it.

**The conversation:**

"Are you coming to practice today?" asked Coach Mensah.

"I can't," said Kwame. "My mother is sick and I have to look after my brother."

"Then come tomorrow. We play Achimota on Saturday and I need you."

"I'll try, sir. I promise."

**Kofi's report:**

Coach Mensah asked Kwame if he was coming to practice that day. Kwame said that he couldn't, because his mother was sick and he had to look after his brother. The coach told him to come the next day, and explained that they were playing Achimota on Saturday and that he needed him. Kwame promised to try.

**Look at the changes:**
- "today" → "that day", "tomorrow" → "the next day"
- "I" (Kwame) → "he"; "you" (Kwame) → "him"
- "can't" → "couldn't"; "is sick" → "was sick"; "have to" → "had to"
- The last line, "I'll try, sir. I promise," became just "promised to try".

**Try this**
Write the report as Kwame himself would tell it to his mother that evening.

## Direct speech in newspapers and news

Newspapers and radio news use both kinds of speech, and you can see the choices they make.

They use **direct speech** for powerful, exact words from an important person. A quotation is proof that the person really said it.

The Minister said, "Every child in Ghana will have a desk by the end of the year."

They use **indirect speech** to summarise long speeches and ordinary information.

The Minister said that the government had bought thirty thousand new desks and that distribution would begin in the Northern Region.

Notice that in news writing, the reporting verb almost always comes **after** the speech or the speaker, and it is usually plain: "said", "told reporters", "announced".

**Try this**
Find a short news article in a newspaper or on your phone. Underline every piece of direct speech in one colour and every piece of indirect speech in another. Which does the writer use more? Why do you think so?

## Speech in plays and comics

Plays and comics show speech in a different way, and comparing them helps you see what quotation marks really do.

In a **play script**, there are no quotation marks at all. The speaker's name goes on the left, followed by a colon, and the words follow. Instructions for the actors go in brackets.

AMA: (looking at her watch) The bus should have come by now.
KOFI: It's always late on market days.
AMA: Then we'll walk.

In a **comic**, the words go inside a speech bubble, and the tail of the bubble points to the speaker.

In a **story**, we have neither the name on the left nor the bubble. So the quotation marks, the reporting verb and the new line for each speaker do all of that work.

## Speech across languages

Ghana has many languages, and most pupils speak at least two. Reporting speech happens in all of them, but not always the same way.

In **Twi**, a reporter often says *ɔkaa sɛ* ("he/she said that") before the words. In **Ewe**, *egblɔ be* does the same job. In **Ga**, *ekɛɛ akɛ*. In each case there is a small joining word, like English "that", which tells the listener that reported words are coming.

Some things to notice when you move between languages:

- English changes the **tense** in indirect speech (am → was). Many Ghanaian languages do not; the verb stays as the speaker said it, and the joining word carries the meaning.
- English changes **pronouns** (I → she). All languages do this, because the reporter is a different person.
- Written English uses **quotation marks**. In spoken language, people use their **voice**: a pause, a change of tone, or a copy of the speaker's accent.

When you write English, you must do with punctuation what your voice does when you speak. That is why the rules in this book matter.

**Try this**
Say "Kofi said he was coming" in your home language. Does the verb change? Does a joining word appear?

## Key words

**Direct speech** – the speaker's exact words, inside quotation marks.
**Indirect (reported) speech** – the meaning of what was said, without quotation marks.
**Quotation marks / inverted commas / speech marks** – the marks " " or ' ' that enclose direct speech.
**Reporting verb** – the verb that tells us someone spoke: said, asked, shouted, told.
**Reporting clause** – the speaker plus the reporting verb: "said Ama", "the teacher asked".
**Pronoun** – a word that stands for a person: I, you, he, she, we, they.
**Backshift** – moving the verb one tense into the past when reporting: am → was, will → would.
**Action beat** – a small action written beside speech instead of a reporting verb.
**Dialogue** – conversation in a story.

**One-line reminders**
- New speaker, new line.
- Punctuation inside the marks.
- Say something; tell someone something.
- Ask **if** for yes/no questions.
- Tell someone **to** do, or **not to** do.
