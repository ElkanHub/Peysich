## Who decides?

Who decided that school is free at the basic level? Who decides how much a trotro driver pays for a licence, where a new road goes, or what happens to someone who steals? Who decided that you may vote at 18, and not 16 or 21?

The answer in every case is **government**: the people and institutions that make rules for the country, carry them out and settle disputes about them.

Ghana is a **republic** and a **democracy**. A republic means the head of state is an elected President, not a king or queen. A democracy means the people choose, through elections, those who govern them, and can remove them.

In this book you will learn:

- what the **1992 Constitution** is and why it matters
- the **three arms** of government and how they check one another
- how the **President** and **Parliament** are chosen and what they do
- how the **courts** work
- how **elections** are run
- how your **district assembly** governs your own area
- the **rights** you have and the **duties** you owe

Let us begin with the rule book.
===
## What is a constitution?

A **constitution** is the supreme law of a country: the rule book that sets out how the country is governed, who holds power, how they get it, what they may and may not do, and what rights the people have.

Every other law must agree with it. If Parliament passes a law that goes against the Constitution, the Supreme Court can strike it down.

Think of a football match. The players are the politicians, the referee is the courts, and the **rules of the game** are the constitution. Without rules, the strongest side would simply do as it liked. With rules, everyone knows what is fair and what happens when it is broken.

Ghana's current constitution is the **1992 Constitution**, approved by the people in a **referendum** (a national vote on one question) in April 1992 and brought into force on **7 January 1993**. It created the **Fourth Republic**, the system we live under today.

Why "Fourth"? Because Ghana has had three earlier attempts, each cut short. The next page tells that story.
===
## The road to the Fourth Republic

@image https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/46/Independence_Arch_Accra_Ghana.jpg | The Independence Arch at Black Star Square, Accra, where the republic was proclaimed | Flowizm · CC BY 2.0 | https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Independence_Arch_Accra_Ghana.jpg

Ghana became independent on **6 March 1957** under Kwame Nkrumah, and a **republic** on **1 July 1960** (the First Republic). In 1966 the army and police removed Nkrumah in a **coup d'état**: a takeover by force.

Since then the country swung between elected governments and military rule:

| Period | What happened |
|---|---|
| 1960–66 | First Republic (Nkrumah) ended by coup |
| 1969–72 | Second Republic (Busia) ended by coup |
| 1979–81 | Third Republic (Limann) ended by coup |
| 1981–92 | Military rule under the PNDC |
| 1993– | **Fourth Republic** under the 1992 Constitution |

Military governments are not chosen by the people and cannot easily be removed by them. The people who wrote the 1992 Constitution wanted to stop the cycle. They made coups illegal, protected human rights, spread power across several institutions, and set fixed dates for elections.

Since 1993 Ghana has held nine general elections and power has changed hands between parties peacefully several times. That is why Ghana is often called an example of democracy in Africa.
===
## What is in the Constitution?

The 1992 Constitution has 26 **chapters** and nearly 300 **articles**. You do not need to know them all, but you should know the main parts:

- **Chapter 1** – it is the supreme law; anyone who tries to overthrow it commits treason.
- **Chapter 3** – who is a **citizen**.
- **Chapter 5** – **fundamental human rights and freedoms** (your rights).
- **Chapter 6** – directive principles of state policy (what government should aim for, such as education and health for all).
- **Chapter 7** – **representation of the people** (elections, the Electoral Commission).
- **Chapter 8** – the **Executive** (the President).
- **Chapter 10** – the **Legislature** (Parliament).
- **Chapter 11** – the **Judiciary** (the courts).
- **Chapter 20** – **decentralisation and local government** (district assemblies).
- **Chapter 22** – **chieftaincy** (the National House of Chiefs).

The Constitution can be changed (**amended**), but only with difficulty: some articles need a referendum and a very large majority, so that no single government can bend the rules for itself.
===
## National symbols

@image https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/19/Flag_of_Ghana.svg/960px-Flag_of_Ghana.svg.png | The flag of Ghana, designed by Theodosia Okoh in 1957 | Theodosia Okoh · Public domain | https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Flag_of_Ghana.svg

The Constitution gives Ghana its **national symbols**, which stand for the nation above any party or leader.

**The flag** – red for the blood of those who died for independence; gold for mineral wealth; green for forests and farms; the **black star** for African freedom. Designed by **Theodosia Okoh**.

**The coat of arms** – two eagles hold a shield divided by a green St George's cross. On the shield: a sword and staff (local and traditional government), a castle on the sea (the national government), a cocoa tree (agriculture) and a mine (mineral wealth). Above is the black star; below, the motto **"Freedom and Justice"**.

**The anthem** – *God Bless Our Homeland Ghana*.

**The pledge** – recited at school assemblies, promising to be faithful and loyal to Ghana.

Learning to respect these symbols is part of being a citizen: they belong to all 30 million of us, whichever party we support.

## The three arms of government

@video KwN1wxbZig8 | Arms of Government and their Functions | Civic Education | SS1 | Afrilearn Education

The Constitution divides the work of government into **three arms** (also called branches), each with a different job:

| Arm | Job | Head | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Executive** | carries out the laws and runs the country day to day | the **President** | Jubilee House, Accra |
| **Legislature** | makes the laws and approves the budget | the **Speaker** | Parliament House, Accra |
| **Judiciary** | interprets the laws and settles disputes | the **Chief Justice** | the courts, led by the Supreme Court |

A simple way to remember: the Legislature **makes** the rules, the Executive **applies** them, and the Judiciary **judges** whether they have been followed.

This division is called the **separation of powers**. The idea is old and simple: if one person or group both made the laws and judged who had broken them, there would be nothing to stop them abusing their power. Splitting the jobs means each arm can watch the others.
===
## Checks and balances

@video uUqeAsogI70 | Three Branches of Government | Learn about the executive, legislative, and judicial branches | Learn Bright

Separating powers is only half the idea. The other half is **checks and balances**: each arm has ways to limit the others.

**Parliament checks the President:**
- It must **approve** the people the President chooses as ministers.
- It must **pass the budget** before government can spend money.
- It can **question** ministers and investigate.
- It can **remove** the President for serious misconduct (impeachment), by a two-thirds vote after an inquiry.

**The President checks Parliament:**
- A bill passed by Parliament becomes law only when the President **signs** it. The President can send it back (a **veto**), though Parliament can override the veto with a two-thirds majority.

**The courts check both:**
- The **Supreme Court** can declare a law or a government action **unconstitutional** and cancel it.

**And the others check the courts:**
- Judges are appointed by the President with Parliament's or the Judicial Council's involvement, and can be removed only for proven misbehaviour.

No arm is all-powerful. That is the point.
===
## Recap: the rule book and the three arms

- A **constitution** is the supreme law. Ghana's is the **1992 Constitution**, in force since **7 January 1993**, creating the **Fourth Republic**.
- It was written to end the cycle of **coups** and protect **rights**.
- Government is divided into **three arms**:
  - **Executive** – President – carries out laws
  - **Legislature** – Parliament, led by the Speaker – makes laws
  - **Judiciary** – courts, led by the Chief Justice – interprets laws
- **Separation of powers** keeps the jobs apart; **checks and balances** let each arm limit the others.
- National symbols (flag, coat of arms, anthem, pledge) belong to all citizens.

## The President

@image https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/61/Golden_Jubilee_House.jpg/960px-Golden_Jubilee_House.jpg | Jubilee House in Accra, the seat of the President | Jessica Gardner · CC BY 2.0 | https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Golden_Jubilee_House.jpg

The **President** is both **head of state** (the symbol of the nation, like a king in some countries) and **head of government** (the person in charge of running it). In many countries these are two different people; in Ghana they are one.

**Chosen how?** By **direct election** of all Ghanaian voters, every four years in December.

**For how long?** A **four-year term**, and a person may serve **no more than two terms**. This limit stops anyone staying in power for life.

**Who may stand?** A citizen of Ghana by birth, at least **40 years old**, and qualified to be elected as an MP.

**To win** a candidate needs **more than 50 %** of the valid votes. If no one reaches that, the top two go to a **run-off** three weeks later. This happened in 2000 and 2008.

**Oath** – the new President is sworn in on **7 January** at Black Star Square, promising to uphold the Constitution.

The **Vice-President** is elected on the same ticket and takes over if the President dies, resigns or is removed.
===
## What the President does

The President's powers are set out in Chapter 8 of the Constitution. The main ones:

- **Appoints ministers** to run the ministries (Education, Health, Finance, Roads, and so on), with Parliament's approval. Most ministers must be MPs.
- **Appoints** the Chief Justice and other senior judges, heads of the army and police, ambassadors, and the chief executives of regions and districts.
- **Commands the armed forces** as Commander-in-Chief.
- **Signs bills** into law, or sends them back to Parliament.
- **Represents Ghana** abroad, signs treaties (which Parliament must ratify) and receives foreign leaders.
- **Presents the budget** through the Minister of Finance.
- **Grants pardons** to convicted people.

The President is helped by:

- the **Cabinet** – the senior ministers who meet to decide policy;
- the **Council of State** – a small body of experienced citizens, including former heads of key institutions and regional representatives, who **advise** the President.

Power with limits: the President cannot make laws alone, spend money Parliament has not approved, or ignore a court ruling.
===
## Ministries and the civil service

The President and ministers decide **what** should be done. The **civil service** does it. Civil servants are permanent, professional staff who stay in post whichever party wins, and who run the day-to-day work of the state.

Examples:

- The **Ministry of Education** sets policy; the **Ghana Education Service** (GES) runs the schools and employs the teachers.
- The **Ministry of Health** sets policy; the **Ghana Health Service** runs hospitals and clinics.
- The **Ministry of Finance** prepares the budget; the **Ghana Revenue Authority** collects taxes.
- The **Ministry of Roads and Highways** plans; the **Ghana Highway Authority** builds and maintains.

Other important bodies work **independently** of ministers so they cannot be pushed around: the **Electoral Commission**, the **Auditor-General** (checks how public money is spent), the **Commission on Human Rights and Administrative Justice** (CHRAJ) and the **Bank of Ghana**.

## Parliament

@image https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/89/Parliament_House_%28State_House%29_%E2%80%93_Parliament_of_Ghana.jpg | Parliament House in Accra, where the laws of Ghana are made | Wgsohne · CC BY-SA 3.0 | https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Parliament_House_%28State_House%29_%E2%80%93_Parliament_of_Ghana.jpg

**Parliament** is Ghana's **legislature**, the law-making body. It has **one chamber** (it is *unicameral*), unlike countries such as Nigeria or the United Kingdom that have two.

**Members** – Ghana is divided into **constituencies**, each electing one **Member of Parliament (MP)**. There are 276 constituencies following the 2024 general election. Voters choose their MP on the same day they choose the President.

**Term** – four years. The whole Parliament is elected together.

**Who may stand** – a citizen aged at least **21**, registered to vote, who has lived in or is from the constituency area and is not disqualified (for example by bankruptcy or a serious conviction).

**The Speaker** – elected by MPs at the first sitting; chairs debates, keeps order and is not required to be an MP. The Speaker is the **third-highest** office in the land after the President and Vice-President.

**Majority and Minority** – the party with more seats forms the Majority and usually supports the President; the Minority holds the government to account.

Parliament meets at **Parliament House**, in the State House grounds, Accra.
===
## What Parliament does

Parliament has four main jobs.

**1. Making laws.** Only Parliament can pass **Acts**. The Free SHS policy, the law creating the NHIS, the Right to Information Act, the Cybersecurity Act: all began as bills in Parliament.

**2. Controlling public money.** Government cannot **tax** or **spend** without Parliament's approval. Each November the Minister of Finance presents the **budget**, and MPs debate and vote on it. Parliament also approves loans and large contracts.

**3. Scrutiny: holding government to account.** MPs ask ministers **questions**, debate their decisions, and sit on **committees** (Education, Health, Public Accounts, and so on) that examine ministries in detail. The **Public Accounts Committee** goes through the Auditor-General's reports and questions officials about misused funds in public.

**4. Representing the people.** Each MP speaks for a constituency, brings its problems to Accra, and helps its development through the MPs' Common Fund.

MPs also **approve the President's nominees** for minister and senior posts, and can **remove** the President or judges in extreme cases.
===
## How a law is made

@video eoQQNTETVLU | How a Law Is Made in Ghana (Step 1) - ACEPA Explains Series | African Centre for Parliamentary Affairs (ACEPA)

A proposed law is a **bill**. It becomes an **Act** by these steps:

1. **Drafting** – usually a ministry writes it, with the Attorney-General's lawyers. The bill is **published in the Gazette** at least 14 days before it comes to Parliament, so the public can read it.
2. **First reading** – the bill is introduced; its title is read. No debate.
3. **Committee stage** – the relevant committee studies it line by line, invites experts and the public, and reports back with suggested changes.
4. **Second reading** – MPs debate the **principles** of the bill and vote on whether to continue.
5. **Consideration stage** – the whole House goes through the bill clause by clause and votes on **amendments**.
6. **Third reading** – a final vote on the whole bill.
7. **Presidential assent** – the President signs within seven days, or sends it back with reasons. If two-thirds of MPs pass it again, it becomes law anyway.
8. **Gazetting** – the new Act is published and comes into force.

Urgent bills can move faster under a **certificate of urgency**, but the steps are the same.
===

## The Judiciary

@image https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a9/Supreme_Court_of_Ghana.jpg/960px-Supreme_Court_of_Ghana.jpg | The Supreme Court of Ghana in Accra | Guido Sohne · CC BY-SA 2.0 | https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Supreme_Court_of_Ghana.jpg

The **Judiciary** is the system of **courts** and **judges**. Its job is to **interpret** the law (say what it means), **apply** it to real cases, and **settle disputes**, between citizens, between a citizen and the state, and between arms of government.

The Constitution says the Judiciary is **independent**: no President, minister or MP may tell a judge how to decide a case. Judges are protected so they can be fearless:

- They are appointed on **merit** by the President, acting on the advice of the **Judicial Council** and, for the Supreme Court, with Parliament's approval.
- They hold office until retirement (70 for the Supreme Court) and can be removed only for **stated misbehaviour or incapacity**, after an inquiry.
- Their salaries cannot be cut while in office.

The head of the Judiciary is the **Chief Justice**, who is also the senior judge of the Supreme Court and manages the whole court system.

Why does independence matter? Because the state is often **one side** in a case. A judge who could be sacked by the President could not judge the President fairly.
===
## The courts, from top to bottom

Ghana's courts form a **ladder**. A case usually begins at the bottom; a party who thinks the decision was wrong can **appeal** to the next rung.

**Superior courts**

- **Supreme Court** – the highest court. It is the final court of appeal and the **only** court that can interpret the Constitution. It decided the 2012 and 2020 presidential election petitions.
- **Court of Appeal** – hears appeals from the High Court.
- **High Court** – hears serious criminal cases (murder, armed robbery) and large civil cases; also protects human rights. There are divisions for commercial, land, labour and human-rights cases.

**Lower courts**

- **Circuit Courts** – mid-level criminal and civil cases.
- **District Courts** – minor offences, small debts, family matters; the court most citizens meet. **Juvenile Courts** sit here for children in trouble.

Alongside these are **Regional Tribunals** and, for chieftaincy disputes, the **Judicial Committees** of the Houses of Chiefs.

Every accused person has the right to a **fair and public** hearing and is **innocent until proven guilty**.
===
## Justice in daily life

The courts are not only for politicians and big companies. Here is how the system touches ordinary people.

- A **land dispute** between two families in a village goes to the High Court's land division, or first to the chief's court if both agree.
- A trader who is **not paid** for goods can sue in the District Court for the money.
- A **worker sacked unfairly** can take the employer to the Labour Division of the High Court.
- Someone **arrested** must be taken before a court within **48 hours** or released; this is a right in Article 14 of the Constitution.
- A pupil's parents who believe the child was **unfairly treated** by a public body can complain to **CHRAJ**, which investigates for free.
- **Legal Aid** gives free lawyers to those who cannot pay.

Justice has problems too: cases can take years, courts are far from many villages, and lawyers are expensive. Reforms such as **e-justice** (filing cases online) and more District Courts aim to fix this.

## Recap: the Judiciary

- The **Judiciary** interprets and applies the law and settles disputes. Its head is the **Chief Justice**.
- Judges are **independent**: appointed on merit, secure in office, free from orders by the other arms.
- The courts are a **ladder**: District and Circuit Courts → High Court → Court of Appeal → **Supreme Court**, the final court and the only one that interprets the Constitution.
- Everyone is **innocent until proven guilty**, has the right to a **fair hearing**, and must be brought to court within **48 hours** of arrest.
- **CHRAJ** and **Legal Aid** help citizens who cannot afford lawyers.

## Elections: the people choose

@image https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/93/Ballot_Box_in_a_Ghana_Election_02.jpg/960px-Ballot_Box_in_a_Ghana_Election_02.jpg | A ballot box at a polling station during a Ghanaian election | Owula kpakpo · CC BY-SA 4.0 | https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ballot_Box_in_a_Ghana_Election_02.jpg

An **election** is how the people choose who governs them. In Ghana the big one is the **general election**, held every four years on **7 December**, when voters choose both the **President** and their **MP** on the same day.

Other elections:

- **District assembly and unit committee elections**, also every four years, but on a different date and **without political parties**.
- **By-elections**, held when an MP dies or resigns between general elections.
- **Referendums**, when the people vote on a single question, such as changing the Constitution.

Who may vote? Any Ghanaian citizen aged **18 or over**, of sound mind, who has **registered** and holds a **voter ID card**. Voting is a **right**, not a duty; nobody may force you to vote or tell you how to vote. The ballot is **secret**.

Elections in Ghana are keenly fought between the two largest parties, the **NDC** and the **NPP**, with smaller parties and independents also standing. Since 1992, power has changed hands between parties four times, each time peacefully.
===
## The Electoral Commission

Elections are run by the **Electoral Commission (EC)**, an **independent** body created by the Constitution. It has seven members, led by a **Chairperson**, appointed by the President in consultation with the Council of State and protected from removal like a judge.

The EC's jobs:

- **Register** voters and keep the voters' register up to date.
- **Draw constituency boundaries** and review them after each census.
- **Register political parties** and make sure they follow the rules.
- **Educate** the public about voting.
- **Conduct** all public elections and referendums and **declare** the results.

The EC must treat all parties equally. Party **agents** watch every polling station and sign the result sheets, so the count is seen by everyone. Observers from Ghanaian groups (such as CODEO) and from abroad also watch.

If a party believes the presidential result was wrong, it may **petition the Supreme Court**, as happened in 2012 and 2020. Both times the Court heard the case in public and the losing side accepted the ruling. That is the rule of law working.
===
## Election day, step by step

@image https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/bf/Ghana_Election_2016_B002a_P03.jpg/960px-Ghana_Election_2016_B002a_P03.jpg | Voters queue to cast their ballots in the 2016 general election | U.S. Embassy in Ghana · Public domain | https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ghana_Election_2016_B002a_P03.jpg

Here is what happens at a **polling station**, often a school classroom, on 7 December.

1. **Opening** at 7 a.m. The presiding officer shows party agents that the ballot boxes are **empty**, then seals them.
2. **Identification** – the voter shows a voter ID card; the biometric machine checks the fingerprint; the name is ticked off the register.
3. **Ink** – a finger is marked with indelible ink so nobody can vote twice.
4. **Two ballot papers** – one for President, one for MP. In the booth, alone, the voter **thumbprints** beside one candidate on each.
5. **The boxes** – the papers go into the correct boxes in full view.
6. **Closing** at 5 p.m. Anyone still in the queue may vote.
7. **Counting** happens **at the polling station**, in public. Agents sign the result sheet (the "pink sheet"), and a copy is posted on the wall.
8. Results travel to the constituency **collation centre**, then to the EC in Accra, which **declares** the winners.

Counting in public at every station is one reason Ghana's results are trusted.
===
## Political parties

A **political party** is a group of people with shared ideas about how the country should be run, who put up candidates for election. Parties:

- choose candidates for President and Parliament
- write a **manifesto** (their promises) for voters to judge
- campaign and explain their ideas
- organise MPs to work together in Parliament
- in opposition, criticise government and offer alternatives

The Constitution says parties must be **national**: they must have members in every region, cannot be based on one ethnic group or religion, and must publish their accounts.

Ghana's two largest parties are the **NDC** (umbrella symbol) and the **NPP** (elephant). Others include the **CPP**, **PNC** and newer movements. Independents may stand without any party.

A healthy democracy needs a strong **opposition**: without it, no one asks hard questions. The Constitution gives the Minority in Parliament a formal role for exactly this reason.

## Recap: elections

- Ghana holds a **general election** every four years on **7 December** for the **President** and all **MPs**.
- Voters must be citizens aged **18+** with a voter ID. Voting is **secret** and **free**.
- The **Electoral Commission**, an independent body, runs elections: registering voters, drawing boundaries, registering parties, conducting the vote and declaring results.
- Counting is done **in public at each polling station**, with party agents signing the results.
- Disputes about the presidential result go to the **Supreme Court**.
- **Political parties** must be national, write manifestos and provide candidates; a strong **opposition** keeps government honest.

## Local government: bringing power closer

@image https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2d/Kumbungu_District_Assembly_%282%29.jpg/960px-Kumbungu_District_Assembly_%282%29.jpg | Kumbungu District Assembly building, Northern Region | Dnshitobu · CC BY-SA 4.0 | https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kumbungu_District_Assembly_%282%29.jpg

Accra cannot know whether the bridge in your village has collapsed or the market in your town needs a new shed. So the Constitution requires **decentralisation**: moving some power and money from the centre to **local** bodies that are close to the people.

Ghana has **16 regions**, each with a **Regional Minister** appointed by the President and a **Regional Coordinating Council** that oversees the districts.

Below the regions are **261 Metropolitan, Municipal and District Assemblies** (MMDAs):

- **Metropolitan** – the biggest cities (population over 250,000), such as Accra, Kumasi, Tamale.
- **Municipal** – large towns (over 95,000), such as Ho, Tema, Sunyani.
- **District** – everywhere else (over 75,000).

Each assembly is the **highest political authority** in its area. It is both a mini-legislature (it passes **by-laws**) and a mini-executive (it builds and runs services). It is the part of government you are most likely to see at work: the public toilet, the market, the borehole, the feeder road.
===
## Who sits in the assembly?

A **District Assembly** is made up of:

- **Elected assembly members** – **70 %** of the seats. Each **electoral area** (a group of communities) elects one member, every four years, on a **non-partisan** basis: candidates stand as individuals, not for a party.
- **Appointed members** – **30 %**, chosen by the President in consultation with chiefs and local groups, to bring in skills such as doctors, teachers, engineers and representatives of women and youth.
- The **MP(s)** for the area, who sit but do not vote.
- The **District Chief Executive**.

The **District Chief Executive (DCE)** (or Metropolitan or Municipal Chief Executive) is the head of the assembly's executive. The President **nominates** the DCE, and **two-thirds** of the assembly members present must **approve** the nominee. The DCE chairs the **Executive Committee**, which carries out the assembly's decisions.

The **Presiding Member** is elected by the assembly from among its members to chair meetings, as the Speaker does in Parliament.

Below the assembly are **Unit Committees**, elected in each community, the lowest level of government and the closest to you.
===
## What the district assembly does

The assembly plans and provides for the **development** of its area. Its duties include:

- **Education** – building and maintaining basic school classrooms, supplying furniture; the GES provides teachers.
- **Health** – clinics and CHPS compounds, sanitation, refuse collection, public toilets.
- **Water** – boreholes and small-town water systems.
- **Roads** – feeder roads, streetlights, drains.
- **Markets and lorry parks** – building and managing them, collecting tolls.
- **Planning** – approving building permits, zoning land, naming streets.
- **By-laws** – local rules on, for example, noise, sanitation and market days, enforced by assembly guards.
- **Disaster response**, working with NADMO.

**Where does the money come from?**

- the **District Assemblies Common Fund** – at least 5 % of national revenue, shared among all assemblies by Parliament's formula;
- **internally generated funds** – property rates, market tolls, business licences, building permits;
- grants from ministries and donors.

## Chiefs and traditional authority

Long before the republic, Ghana's peoples governed themselves through **chiefs**: the Asantehene in Kumasi, the Ga Mantse in Accra, the Ya-Na in Yendi, the Awoamefia of Anlo, and thousands of paramount, divisional and village chiefs.

The 1992 Constitution **protects chieftaincy** (Chapter 22). Chiefs are chosen by **custom**, not election, and may not take part in **party politics**, which keeps them above the divisions of elections.

Chiefs still matter:

- They are **custodians of land** in much of the country; developments need their consent.
- They **settle disputes** in their courts, especially about land, marriage and custom.
- They **mobilise** communities for development: communal labour, school building, festivals.
- They advise the district assembly, and sit in the **Regional** and **National Houses of Chiefs**, which rule on chieftaincy disputes and record customary law.

Modern and traditional government work side by side. The DCE and the paramount chief of the same area will often share a platform at a durbar, each with a different kind of authority.
===
## Recap: local government

- **Decentralisation** moves power close to the people: **16 regions**, **261 MMDAs**, and unit committees.
- A **District Assembly** has **70 % elected** members (non-partisan, four-year terms) and **30 % appointed** by the President.
- The **DCE** is nominated by the President and approved by two-thirds of the assembly; the **Presiding Member** chairs meetings.
- Assemblies build and run schools, clinics, water, roads, markets and sanitation, pass **by-laws** and plan land use.
- Money comes from the **Common Fund**, local **rates and tolls**, and grants.
- **Chiefs** are protected by the Constitution, chosen by custom, outside party politics, and important for land, disputes and development.

## Your rights: Chapter 5

@video O5D_TvzuR3w | Knowledge is power!, Get to know the Constitution of Ghana | Make It Legal

Chapter 5 of the Constitution lists the **fundamental human rights and freedoms** of everyone in Ghana. They belong to you by being human; the state did not give them and may not take them away without very good reason set out in law.

The main rights:

- **Life** (Art. 13) – no one may be killed except by a court sentence after a fair trial.
- **Personal liberty** (Art. 14) – no arrest without reason; court within 48 hours.
- **Human dignity** (Art. 15) – no torture or degrading treatment.
- **Equality and freedom from discrimination** (Art. 17) – on grounds of gender, race, ethnic origin, religion, creed or social status.
- **Privacy** (Art. 18) of home and correspondence.
- **Fair trial** (Art. 19) – innocent until proven guilty, told the charge, allowed a lawyer.
- **Property** (Art. 20) – no taking of property without fair payment.
- **Freedoms** (Art. 21) – of speech, the press, thought, religion, assembly, association, movement, and to take part in public life.
- **Education** (Art. 25) – free, compulsory basic education; secondary education made progressively free.
- **Women's rights** (Art. 27), **children's rights** (Art. 28), **rights of disabled persons** (Art. 29).
===
## Children's rights

Article 28 of the Constitution is about **you**. It says Parliament must make laws so that every child has:

- the right to the **care of parents** and, if parents fail, the care of the state;
- **protection from work** that threatens health, education or development;
- protection from **physical or moral harm**;
- the right to **medical treatment**, whatever the parents' religion;
- protection from being deprived of liberty except by a court;
- **no discrimination** because of the circumstances of birth.

The **Children's Act (1998)** fills these out: the minimum age for ordinary work is **15**, for hazardous work **18**; a child must not be married under 18; every child has a right to a name, a nationality, education and to express an opinion on matters that affect them.

**Who protects these rights?** Parents first; then schools, the Department of Social Welfare, the police's **DOVVSU** unit, **CHRAJ**, and the courts.

## Rights come with duties

@image https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/59/Coat_of_arms_of_Ghana.svg/960px-Coat_of_arms_of_Ghana.svg.png | The coat of arms of Ghana, with the motto Freedom and Justice | Sodacan · Public domain | https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Coat_of_arms_of_Ghana.svg

Rights are not free-standing. If you have a right to education, someone must pay the teacher; if you have a right to safety, someone must obey the law. **Article 41** of the Constitution lists the **duties of a citizen**:

- to **promote the prestige and good name** of Ghana and respect its symbols;
- to **uphold the Constitution** and the law;
- to foster **national unity** and live in harmony with others;
- to **respect the rights and freedoms** of others;
- to **work conscientiously** in a lawful occupation;
- to **protect and preserve public property** and expose waste and misuse;
- to **contribute to the well-being** of the community;
- to **defend Ghana** and render national service when required;
- to **co-operate with lawful agencies** in maintaining law and order;
- to **declare income honestly** and pay **taxes**;
- to **protect the environment**.

Every right you enjoy is someone else's duty, and every duty you perform protects someone else's right.
===
## Being a good citizen now

You do not have to wait until 18 to be a citizen. Here is what citizenship looks like for a Basic 8 pupil:

**At school**
- Come on time; do the work. Free basic education is a right paid for by taxpayers.
- Look after textbooks, desks and the school's property.
- Treat everyone fairly: no bullying, no mocking anyone's ethnic group, religion or disability.
- Take part: class prefect elections, debate club, clean-up days.

**In the community**
- Join communal labour.
- Do not litter; dispose of rubbish properly. Choked gutters flood whole neighbourhoods.
- Report dangers (a broken wire, an open manhole) to an adult or the assembly member.
- Respect elders and chiefs, and expect to be respected in return.

**As a future voter**
- Follow the news. Learn the difference between a fact and a rumour, and check before you share.
- Register to vote when you turn 18.
- Judge leaders by what they do, not by their party, their region or what they give out at election time.

## Corruption and accountability

**Corruption** is using public power for private gain: a bribe to pass an inspection, a contract given to a friend, a ghost name on the payroll. It steals from everyone: a stolen cedi is a desk, a drug or a kilometre of road that never arrives.

The Constitution and laws set up **watchdogs**:

- the **Auditor-General** – examines all public accounts and reports to Parliament;
- the **Public Accounts Committee** – questions officials about the reports, in public;
- **CHRAJ** – investigates corruption and abuse of office;
- the **Office of the Special Prosecutor** – prosecutes corruption cases;
- **EOCO** – investigates economic and organised crime;
- the **Right to Information Act (2019)** – lets any citizen ask a public body for information, and get it;
- a **free press**, protected by Article 162, which exposes wrongdoing.

**Accountability** means those with power must answer for how they use it. Elections are the biggest form: every four years the people can say no.

You play a part too: refusing to pay or offer a bribe, and speaking up when you see one, is citizenship in action.
===
## Paying for government: the budget

Schools, hospitals, roads, judges' salaries, the army: all of it costs money. Where does government get it?

**Taxes** – the largest source:
- **Income tax (PAYE)** taken from salaries;
- **VAT** and levies added to the price of goods and services;
- **Import duties** paid at Tema and Takoradi ports and at borders;
- **Corporate tax** paid by companies on their profits;
- **Property rates** and **tolls** collected by assemblies.

**Non-tax revenue** – royalties from gold, oil and cocoa; fees for passports and licences; profits of state companies.

**Borrowing** – loans from banks, from other countries and from bodies such as the World Bank and IMF. Loans must be repaid with interest, so Parliament must approve each one.

Each November the Minister of Finance presents the **budget** to Parliament: how much will be raised and how it will be spent. MPs debate it line by line. Once passed, the **Appropriation Act** gives government permission to spend.

Paying tax is a duty in Article 41. A citizen who dodges tax is taking a service without paying for it.
===
## Ghana in the world

Government does not stop at the border. Ghana belongs to international bodies that shape its laws and daily life.

**ECOWAS** (Economic Community of West African States) – fifteen West African countries, headquartered in Abuja. Citizens may travel and trade across member states with an ECOWAS passport, which is why Nigerian and Togolese traders are common in Ghana's markets and Ghanaians in theirs. The **ECOWAS Court** can hear human-rights cases from Ghanaian citizens.

**The African Union (AU)** – all African states, headquartered in Addis Ababa; Ghana was a founder of its predecessor, the OAU, in 1963. Ghana hosts the secretariat of the **African Continental Free Trade Area** in Accra.

**The United Nations (UN)** – Ghana joined in 1957, has served on the Security Council, and is one of the largest contributors of **peacekeeping** troops. Kofi Annan, a Ghanaian, was UN Secretary-General from 1997 to 2006.

**The Commonwealth** – countries once ruled by Britain, sharing English, parliamentary traditions and the Commonwealth Games.

Treaties Ghana signs must be **ratified by Parliament** before they bind the country. International law thus enters through the same door as every other law.
===
## Recap: rights and duties

- **Chapter 5** guarantees fundamental human rights: life, liberty, dignity, equality, privacy, fair trial, property, freedoms of speech, religion, assembly and movement, and education.
- **Article 28** and the **Children's Act** protect children from harmful work, early marriage and harm, and guarantee care, health and education.
- **Article 41** lists the **duties** of a citizen: obey the law, respect others' rights, work honestly, protect public property, pay taxes, protect the environment, defend Ghana.
- **CHRAJ**, the **Auditor-General**, the **Special Prosecutor** and a **free press** hold power to account; **elections** are the people's final check.

## Glossary

**Constitution** — the supreme law setting out how a country is governed and the rights of its people.

**Republic** — a state whose head is an elected President, not a monarch.

**Democracy** — government chosen by the people through free elections.

**Coup d'état** — the seizure of government by force, usually by the military.

**Executive / Legislature / Judiciary** — the three arms: carrying out laws, making laws, interpreting laws.

**Separation of powers** — dividing those three jobs among different bodies.

**Checks and balances** — the powers each arm has to limit the others.

**Bill / Act** — a proposed law / a law passed by Parliament and signed by the President.

**Constituency** — the area that elects one MP.

**Run-off** — a second presidential vote between the top two candidates.

**Referendum** — a national vote on a single question.

**Decentralisation** — moving power and money from central government to local bodies.

**By-law** — a local rule made by a district assembly.

**CHRAJ** — Commission on Human Rights and Administrative Justice.

**Impeachment** — the process by which Parliament can remove a President.
