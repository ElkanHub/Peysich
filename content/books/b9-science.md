## Electricity in your day

Before Kofi leaves for school in Tamale, he has already used electricity a dozen times: the bulb in the bathroom, the fan that ran all night, the phone he charged, the fridge that kept the water cold, the radio playing the morning news.

Electricity is so ordinary that we only notice it when the lights go off. Yet most people cannot say what is actually moving inside the wire, why a bulb glows, or why a fuse blows.

This book answers those questions. You will learn:

- what **current**, **voltage** and **resistance** are, and how they are measured,
- how to draw and build **series** and **parallel** circuits,
- why some materials **conduct** and others **insulate**,
- how to stay **safe** with electricity at home,
- how the **ECG bill** is calculated, and how to lower it.

Every idea here appears in the BECE Integrated Science paper. Just as important, every idea could one day keep you or your family safe.

## Where Ghana's electricity comes from

Electricity is made in **power stations** and sent through wires to homes. In Ghana, about a third comes from **hydroelectric** dams on the Volta River: Akosombo, Kpong and Bui. Falling water spins a turbine, the turbine spins a generator, and the generator pushes electric charge along the wires.

Most of the rest comes from **thermal** plants near Tema and Takoradi, which burn natural gas to spin their turbines. A small but growing share comes from **solar** farms in the north, such as at Kaleo and Bui.

The electricity leaves the station at very high voltage along **GRIDCo** transmission lines on tall pylons. Near your town, a **transformer** lowers the voltage, and the **Electricity Company of Ghana (ECG)** or **NEDCo** delivers it to your house at 230 volts.

## Charge: the thing that moves

Everything is made of atoms. Each atom has a heavy centre (the nucleus) with **positive** charge, surrounded by tiny **electrons** with **negative** charge.

In metals, some electrons are not tied to any one atom. They drift about freely inside the metal, like people in a crowded market. These are **free electrons**, and they are what moves when a current flows.

Charge is measured in **coulombs (C)**. One coulomb is a very large number of electrons: about six million million million of them.

Two rules about charge:

- **Like charges repel**, unlike charges attract.
- Charge is never created or destroyed; it only moves from place to place.

When you rub a balloon on your hair and it sticks to the wall, you have moved a few electrons from one surface to another. That is **static** electricity. A circuit, by contrast, keeps the charge moving steadily. That is **current** electricity, the subject of this book.
===
## Current: charge on the move

**Electric current** is the flow of electric charge. In a wire it is the free electrons drifting along together.

Current is measured in **amperes**, or **amps (A)**. One amp means one coulomb of charge passing a point every second.

**I = Q ÷ t**, where I is current, Q is charge in coulombs and t is time in seconds.

Typical currents:

- Phone charger: about 1 to 2 A
- LED bulb: about 0.05 A
- Electric kettle: about 8 A
- Air conditioner: about 5 to 10 A
- Lightning: tens of thousands of amps

Current is measured with an **ammeter**, which is connected **in the path** of the current, so the current flows through it. We say it is connected **in series**.

**Try this**

If 30 coulombs of charge pass through a bulb in 10 seconds, what is the current?

*Answer: I = 30 ÷ 10 = 3 A.*
===
## Which way does current flow?

Electrons are negative, so they flow **from the negative terminal to the positive terminal** of a battery. That is the **electron flow**.

But long before electrons were discovered, scientists had already agreed that current flows **from positive to negative**. That is called **conventional current**, and it is still the direction we use in circuit diagrams and in every exam.

Do not let this confuse you. Both describe the same thing, seen from opposite sides, like a bus going "to Kumasi" or "from Accra". For all calculations in this book and in the BECE, use **conventional current: positive to negative**.

The arrow on a diode or a current label in a diagram always shows conventional current.

**Try this**

A battery has its positive terminal on the left and negative on the right. Draw a simple loop with one bulb, and add an arrow showing conventional current. Then add a smaller dotted arrow showing electron flow.

## Voltage: the push

Current does not flow by itself. Something must push it. That push is **voltage**, also called **potential difference (p.d.)**.

A battery or generator gives energy to the charges. Voltage measures **how much energy each coulomb of charge carries**. It is measured in **volts (V)**. One volt means one joule of energy for every coulomb.

Think of water in a tank on a roof. The height of the tank is like the voltage: the higher the tank, the harder the water pushes out of the tap. The flow from the tap is like current.

Typical voltages:

- Torch cell: 1.5 V
- Phone battery: 3.7 V
- Car battery: 12 V
- Ghana mains supply: 230 V
- GRIDCo transmission lines: 161,000 V

Voltage is measured with a **voltmeter**, connected **across** a component, one lead on each side. We say it is connected **in parallel** with the component.

## Resistance: the opposition

As electrons push through a wire, they bump into the atoms of the metal. This opposition to the flow is called **resistance**, measured in **ohms (Ω)**.

A thick copper wire has very low resistance; current flows easily. A thin wire, a long wire, or a wire made of a poorer conductor has higher resistance.

Resistance is not always a nuisance. The thin coiled wire inside an old-style bulb has high resistance; it gets so hot that it glows white. The heating element in a kettle or an iron works the same way. A **resistor** is a component made on purpose to have a fixed resistance, used to control current in radios, phones and chargers.

Four things increase the resistance of a wire:

1. Greater **length**.
2. Smaller **thickness** (cross-section).
3. A **material** that conducts poorly.
4. Higher **temperature** (for metals).

**Try this**

Two copper wires are the same length. One is twice as thick as the other. Which has the lower resistance?

## Ohm's law

In 1827 Georg Ohm found a simple relationship. For a metal wire at steady temperature, the current is **directly proportional** to the voltage across it. Double the voltage, and you double the current.

The law is written:

**V = I × R**

Voltage = Current × Resistance.

Rearranged:

- **I = V ÷ R** (to find current)
- **R = V ÷ I** (to find resistance)

The triangle in the picture helps. Cover the quantity you want; what is left shows the formula.

**Example.** A 230 V heater has a resistance of 46 Ω. What current does it draw?

I = V ÷ R = 230 ÷ 46 = **5 A**.

**Example.** A bulb carries 0.5 A when connected to a 12 V battery. Its resistance?

R = V ÷ I = 12 ÷ 0.5 = **24 Ω**.

## A simple circuit

A **circuit** is a complete, closed path that current can flow around. The simplest one has three parts:

1. A **source** of voltage: a cell or battery.
2. A **load** that uses the energy: a bulb, a motor, a buzzer.
3. **Conductors** joining them: wires.

Usually there is also a **switch**, which opens a gap in the path to stop the current, or closes it to let current flow.

If the path is broken anywhere, by an open switch, a cut wire or a blown bulb, current stops **everywhere** in that loop. That is an **open circuit**. When the path is complete, it is a **closed circuit**.

In a working circuit the bulb glows, because its thin wire resists the current and turns electrical energy into heat and light.

## Circuit symbols

Scientists and electricians draw circuits with standard **symbols**, so that anyone in the world can read them. Learn these for the exam.

- **Cell:** a long thin line beside a short thick line. The long line is the positive terminal.
- **Battery:** two or more cells in a row.
- **Switch (open):** a line with a gap, like a drawbridge lifted.
- **Switch (closed):** the gap bridged.
- **Bulb (lamp):** a circle with a cross inside.
- **Resistor:** a rectangle.
- **Variable resistor:** a rectangle with an arrow through it.
- **Ammeter:** a circle with an A.
- **Voltmeter:** a circle with a V.
- **Fuse:** a rectangle with a line through it.
- **Wires:** straight lines, with corners drawn square.

Rules for drawing: use a ruler, keep corners at right angles, do not let wires cross unless they are joined (draw a dot at a junction), and label every component.

## Conductors and insulators

A **conductor** lets current flow easily, because it has many free electrons. An **insulator** does not, because its electrons are held tightly to their atoms.

**Conductors:** all metals, especially silver, copper, gold and aluminium. Also graphite (pencil lead), salty water, and the human body.

**Insulators:** rubber, plastic, glass, dry wood, dry air, ceramic, paper, dry cloth.

Look at any cable: copper in the middle (conductor) wrapped in plastic (insulator). The copper carries the current; the plastic keeps it in and keeps you out.

**Semi-conductors** such as silicon are in between. They are the basis of every chip in your phone, but that is a story for SHS.

**Test it.** Make a circuit with a cell, a bulb and two loose wire ends. Touch the ends to a coin, a plastic ruler, a pencil lead, a piece of chalk, a key. The bulb lights for conductors only.

## Why water is dangerous

Pure water is actually a poor conductor. But the water in your house, in rivers and on your skin is never pure: it carries dissolved salts, and dissolved salts turn water into a conductor.

Your body is mostly salty water, so **you conduct electricity**. Dry skin has a fairly high resistance, which gives a little protection. Wet skin has a much lower resistance, so the same voltage pushes a far bigger current through you.

This is why:

- you must never touch switches or plugs with wet hands,
- bathrooms have pull-cord switches or switches outside the door,
- you must never use a phone that is charging while in the bath,
- flooded rooms must have the power switched off at the main before anyone enters.

A current of as little as **0.05 A (50 milliamps)** through the chest can stop the heart. A mains socket can supply a hundred times that.

## Series circuits

In a **series circuit**, the components are joined one after another in a **single loop**. The current has only one path.

Three rules:

1. **The current is the same everywhere.** An ammeter placed anywhere in the loop reads the same value.
2. **The voltages add up.** The voltage across all the components together equals the battery voltage. V = V₁ + V₂ + V₃.
3. **The resistances add up.** R_total = R₁ + R₂ + R₃.

**Example.** A 12 V battery is connected to a 2 Ω and a 4 Ω resistor in series.

R_total = 2 + 4 = 6 Ω.
I = V ÷ R = 12 ÷ 6 = 2 A, everywhere.
Voltage across 2 Ω: V = IR = 2 × 2 = 4 V.
Voltage across 4 Ω: 2 × 4 = 8 V.
Check: 4 + 8 = 12 V. Correct.

The big drawback: if one component fails, the whole loop is broken, and everything goes off. That is why cheap Christmas lights all die when one bulb blows.
===
## Parallel circuits

In a **parallel circuit**, the components are connected side by side, each on its own **branch**. The current has more than one path.

Three rules:

1. **The voltage is the same across every branch**, and equal to the battery voltage.
2. **The currents in the branches add up** to the total current from the battery. I = I₁ + I₂ + I₃.
3. **The total resistance is less** than the smallest branch resistance. For two resistors: 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂.

**Example.** A 12 V battery feeds a 4 Ω and a 6 Ω resistor in parallel.

Voltage across each: 12 V.
Current in 4 Ω branch: 12 ÷ 4 = 3 A.
Current in 6 Ω branch: 12 ÷ 6 = 2 A.
Total current: 3 + 2 = 5 A.
Total resistance: 12 ÷ 5 = 2.4 Ω. (Less than 4 Ω, as expected.)

If one branch breaks, the others keep working. That is why the lights in your house are wired in parallel: the kitchen bulb does not care whether the bedroom bulb has blown.

## Series or parallel? Comparing them

| | Series | Parallel |
|---|---|---|
| Paths for current | one | two or more |
| Current | same everywhere | splits between branches |
| Voltage | shared between components | same across each branch |
| Total resistance | adds up (bigger) | smaller than any branch |
| One component fails | all stop | others keep working |
| Bulbs' brightness | dimmer as more are added | each stays bright |
| Used for | torches, simple switches, fuses | house wiring, car lights |

**Bulb experiment.** Connect two identical bulbs in series to a battery; both glow dimly. Connect the same two in parallel; both glow at full brightness, but the battery runs down faster, because it is supplying more current.

*Answer: in series, all the current passes through the fuse, so it can cut the whole circuit when the current is too high.*

## Measuring with meters

An **ammeter** measures current. It must be placed **in series**, so that the current flows through it. A good ammeter has almost zero resistance, so it does not change the current it is measuring.

A **voltmeter** measures voltage across a component. It is placed **in parallel**, one lead on each side of the component. A good voltmeter has very high resistance, so almost no current is diverted through it.

Connect them the wrong way round and you can damage them: an ammeter in parallel across a battery is almost a short circuit.

A **multimeter** combines both, plus an ohmmeter for resistance. Turn the dial to the quantity you want, choose a range higher than the value you expect, and connect the red lead to the positive side.

## Electrical power

**Power** is how fast energy is used. It is measured in **watts (W)**. One watt is one joule of energy every second. A thousand watts is a **kilowatt (kW)**.

For electrical appliances:

**P = V × I**

Power = Voltage × Current.

**Example.** A fan on the 230 V mains draws 0.3 A. Its power is 230 × 0.3 = **69 W**.

**Example.** A 2,300 W water heater on 230 V draws I = P ÷ V = 2,300 ÷ 230 = **10 A**. That is why heaters need thick cables and their own fuse.

Typical power ratings (look for the label on the back of the appliance):

- LED bulb: 9 W
- Old-style bulb: 60 W
- Television: 80 W
- Fridge: 150 W
- Iron: 1,000 W
- Air conditioner: 1,500 W
- Electric kettle: 2,000 W

Anything that makes **heat** uses a lot of power. That fact will matter when we get to the bill.
===
## Energy and the kilowatt-hour

ECG does not charge you for power; it charges you for **energy**: power multiplied by time.

**Energy = Power × Time**

The scientific unit is the joule, but a joule is tiny. Electricity companies use a bigger unit, the **kilowatt-hour (kWh)**, often just called a **unit**. One kWh is the energy used by a 1 kW appliance running for 1 hour.

**Energy (kWh) = Power (kW) × Time (hours)**

**Example.** A 2 kW kettle used for 15 minutes (0.25 h): 2 × 0.25 = **0.5 kWh**.

**Example.** A 9 W LED bulb left on for 10 hours: 0.009 × 10 = **0.09 kWh**.

**Example.** A 1.5 kW air conditioner running 8 hours a night: 1.5 × 8 = **12 kWh** each night, or 360 kWh a month. That one appliance can use more than everything else in the house combined.

**Try this**

A 1,000 W iron is used for 2 hours a week. How many kWh does it use in four weeks?

*Answer: 1 × 2 × 4 = 8 kWh.*

## Reading the ECG bill

Abena's family in Cape Coast uses a **prepaid meter**. They buy credit at a vendor or through mobile money, type the token into the meter, and the units appear. When the units run out, the power goes off. A **postpaid** customer gets a bill at the end of the month instead, based on a meter reading.

Either way, the charge is built from:

- **Energy charge:** the number of kWh used × the tariff (the price per kWh). Residential tariffs in Ghana are set by the **PURC** in blocks: the first block of units each month is cheapest, the next block costs more, and heavy users pay the highest rate.
- **Service charge:** a fixed monthly amount.
- **Levies and VAT:** government charges added to the total.

The exact tariff changes from year to year. The method does not.

**Worked example (illustrative tariff).** A home uses 150 kWh in a month. Suppose the first 50 units cost GH₵ 0.80 each and the rest GH₵ 1.50 each, with a service charge of GH₵ 10.

Energy: (50 × 0.80) + (100 × 1.50) = 40 + 150 = GH₵ 190.
Plus service charge: GH₵ 200. Levies would be added on top.

## Cutting the bill

Because the bill depends on **power × time**, there are only two ways to lower it: use less powerful appliances, or use them for less time.

**Swap for lower power**

- Replace old 60 W bulbs with 9 W LEDs: same light, one-sixth of the energy.
- Choose appliances with a high **energy-efficiency star rating** (Ghana's Energy Commission label).
- Use a fan instead of an air conditioner when you can.

**Reduce the time**

- Switch off lights and fans in empty rooms.
- Unplug chargers and the TV at the wall; standby still draws current.
- Iron a week's clothes at once rather than heating the iron every morning.
- Do not open the fridge and stand there deciding; every opening makes the motor run.
- Set the air conditioner to 25 °C, not 18 °C.

**Try this**

A house has ten 60 W bulbs on for 6 hours daily. Calculate the monthly saving in kWh if all are swapped for 9 W LEDs.

*Answer: (0.6 − 0.09) × 6 × 30 = 91.8 kWh.*

- **P = V × I**, in watts. Heating appliances use the most power.

- Save money by lowering power (LEDs, efficient appliances) and lowering time (switch off, unplug).

## The three-pin plug

Look inside a plug (with it unplugged). You will find three wires, each with a job and a colour.

- **Live (brown):** carries the current in from the supply at 230 V. Connected to the right-hand pin, through the **fuse**.
- **Neutral (blue):** carries the current back. Left-hand pin.
- **Earth (green and yellow):** a safety wire. Top, longest pin. It connects the metal casing of the appliance to the ground.

Older Ghanaian wiring sometimes uses the old colours: red for live, black for neutral, green for earth. If you ever see both systems in one house, ask an electrician to check it.

The **cable grip** at the bottom of the plug clamps the outer sheath so that pulling the cable does not pull the wires off their pins.

## Fuses and circuit breakers

A **fuse** is a thin piece of wire inside a small cartridge, placed in series on the live wire. If the current grows too large, because of a fault or an overload, the wire heats up and **melts**, breaking the circuit before the cable catches fire.

Fuses are rated in amps: 3 A, 5 A, 13 A. Choose a fuse slightly **above** the normal current of the appliance. A 69 W fan draws 0.3 A, so a 3 A fuse is right. A 2,300 W heater draws 10 A, so it needs a 13 A fuse. Putting a 13 A fuse on the fan would let a dangerous current flow without blowing.

Never replace a fuse with a nail, a coin or a thick piece of wire. People do this in Ghana when the fuse keeps blowing. The fuse keeps blowing because something is wrong; bypassing it turns a warning into a fire.

A **circuit breaker** does the same job with a switch that trips instead of a wire that melts. It can be reset, which is why modern houses use breakers in the consumer unit (the "mains box").

**Try this**

A microwave is rated 1,150 W on 230 V. What current does it draw, and which fuse (3 A, 5 A or 13 A) should it have?

*Answer: 5 A, so a 13 A fuse (5 A would be too close).*

## Earthing and shocks

Imagine a metal-bodied fridge. A wire inside comes loose and touches the metal casing. The casing is now at 230 V. Touch it, and current flows through you to the ground. That is an **electric shock**.

The **earth wire** prevents this. It connects the casing to the ground through a very low-resistance path. The moment the live wire touches the casing, a huge current rushes down the earth wire, the fuse blows, and the appliance is cut off before anyone touches it.

Appliances with plastic bodies (radios, phone chargers, hair dryers) are **double insulated** and have only two wires; there is no metal to earth.

Shock injuries range from a tingle to burns, to muscles locking so you cannot let go, to the heart stopping. If someone is being shocked: **do not touch them**. Switch off at the mains or knock the wire away with something dry and non-conducting, such as a wooden broom handle. Then call for help.

## Dangers at home

Most electrical accidents in Ghana come from a short list of habits. Know them, and refuse to do them.

1. **Overloading sockets** with multiple adaptors. Each socket is wired for about 13 A. Plug in a heater, an iron and a kettle together and the wires behind the wall can overheat.
2. **Damaged cables** with the copper showing. Replace, do not tape.
3. **Illegal connections** ("by-pass") that skip the meter and the breaker, leaving no protection at all.
4. **Wet hands** on switches and plugs.
5. **Flying kites or climbing trees near power lines.** Overhead lines are bare; a kite string wet with dew conducts.
6. **Building under high-voltage lines.** GRIDCo lines carry 161,000 V; the air itself can conduct over short distances.
7. **Sleeping with a phone charging under the pillow.** Chargers get hot; pillows trap heat.
8. **Children poking sockets.** Use socket covers.

## Electrical fires

An electrical fire starts when a wire or component gets hot enough to ignite something near it. Causes: overloaded sockets, loose connections that spark, bypassed fuses, faulty chargers.

**If an electrical fire starts:**

1. Switch off the power at the mains, if you can reach it safely.
2. **Never pour water** on it while the power is on. Water conducts; you could be electrocuted.
3. Use a dry-powder or CO₂ extinguisher, or smother a small fire with a thick dry blanket or sand.
4. Get everyone out and call the Ghana National Fire Service on **192**.

**Prevent it:**

- One appliance per socket for anything that heats.
- Replace cracked plugs and frayed cables.
- Have wiring older than 20 years checked by a licensed electrician.
- Do not run cables under carpets, where heat cannot escape.
- Switch off and unplug heating appliances when you leave the house.

## Recap 5: safety

- **Plug:** live brown, neutral blue, earth green/yellow. Fuse on the live.
- **Fuse:** melts when current is too high; rate it just above the normal current. Never bypass it.
- **Earth:** gives fault current a safe path so the fuse blows before you are shocked.
- **Shock:** do not touch the victim; cut the power first.
- **Fire:** switch off, no water, extinguisher or sand, call 192.
- **Avoid:** overloading, bare wires, wet hands, kites near lines, illegal connections.

**Quick quiz**

1. Which wire is brown?
2. A 60 W lamp on 230 V: 3 A or 13 A fuse?
3. Why must you not pour water on an electrical fire?

*Answers: live; 3 A; water conducts and could electrocute you.*

## Practical: build a circuit

You need: two 1.5 V cells, a cell holder or tape, three pieces of insulated wire with bare ends, two small torch bulbs with holders, a paper clip and two drawing pins for a switch.

**Series**

1. Join the cells so the positive of one touches the negative of the other.
2. Connect a wire from the battery's positive to bulb 1, bulb 1 to bulb 2, bulb 2 to the switch, and the switch back to the battery's negative.
3. Close the switch. Both bulbs glow dimly. Unscrew one bulb: the other goes out.

**Parallel**

4. Now connect bulb 1 directly across the battery, and bulb 2 also directly across the battery, with the switch on the battery's negative lead.
5. Close the switch. Both bulbs glow brightly. Unscrew one: the other stays lit.

Record what you see in a table: arrangement, brightness, effect of removing one bulb. This is a standard BECE practical.

## Worked BECE-style question

> (a) State Ohm's law. (b) A resistor of 20 Ω is connected to a 10 V battery. Calculate the current. (c) A second resistor of 30 Ω is added in series. Calculate (i) the total resistance, (ii) the new current, (iii) the voltage across the 30 Ω resistor. (d) State one advantage of connecting lamps in parallel in a house.

**(a)** The current through a metallic conductor is directly proportional to the voltage across it, provided the temperature stays constant.

**(b)** I = V ÷ R = 10 ÷ 20 = **0.5 A**.

**(c)(i)** R = 20 + 30 = **50 Ω**. **(ii)** I = 10 ÷ 50 = **0.2 A**. **(iii)** V = IR = 0.2 × 30 = **6 V**.

**(d)** If one lamp fails, the others continue to work (or: each lamp receives the full supply voltage).

Marks come for the statement, the formula, the substitution and the answer with unit. Write all four, every time.

## Revision card

**Quantities:** I (A) = Q ÷ t · V (V) = energy per coulomb · R (Ω) = opposition.

**Ohm's law:** V = I R.

**Power:** P = V I (W). **Energy:** E = P × t (kWh = kW × h).

**Series:** same I · V adds · R adds · one fails, all fail.

**Parallel:** same V · I adds · 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ · one fails, rest work.

**Meters:** ammeter in series, voltmeter in parallel.

**Plug:** live brown (fused), neutral blue, earth green/yellow.

**Bill:** units × tariff (blocks) + service charge + levies. Lower P or lower t.

**Safety:** no wet hands · no overloading · no bypassed fuses · no kites near lines · cut power before helping a shock victim · no water on electrical fires · call 192.
