## Everything is made of something

Look around you. A desk. A cup of water. The air you breathe.

All of these are **matter**. Matter is anything that takes up space and has weight. Your pencil is matter. The sea is matter. Even the air in a balloon is matter, although you cannot see it.

Scientists sort matter into three groups called **states**:

- **Solids**, like stones, wood and ice.
- **Liquids**, like water, palm oil and milk.
- **Gases**, like the air around you and the steam from a pot.

In this book you will learn how to tell the three states apart, and how heating and cooling can change one into another.

**Try this:** Name one solid, one liquid and one gas you can find in your kitchen.

## Solids

A **solid** keeps its own shape.

Put a stone on the table. It stays a stone. Put it in a cup. It is still the same shape. You can pick it up and hold it.

Solids:
- have a fixed **shape**;
- take up a fixed amount of space (a fixed **volume**);
- can be hard (a rock) or soft (a sponge), but they still keep their shape.

Examples: a wooden stool, a coin, a book, a piece of chalk, a kenkey ball, a brick, your bones.

Some solids can be bent or squashed, like rubber or cloth, but they do not flow and they do not spread out to fill a container.

**Try this:** Find five solids in your classroom. Say what each one is made of.

## Liquids

A **liquid** flows and takes the shape of its container.

Pour water into a cup: it is cup-shaped. Pour the same water into a bowl: now it is bowl-shaped. The amount of water stays the same, but its shape changes.

Liquids:
- have **no fixed shape**;
- take up a fixed amount of space (fixed volume);
- can be **poured**;
- are wet.

Examples: water, palm oil, milk, petrol, juice, honey, groundnut soup.

Some liquids flow quickly, like water. Some flow slowly, like honey. Both are liquids.

**Try this:** Pour some water from a cup into a plate. What shape is it now? Pour it back. Did any of the water change?
===
## Gases

A **gas** spreads out to fill all the space it can.

Blow up a balloon. The air you blow in spreads out to fill the whole balloon. Let it go, and the air rushes out and spreads into the room.

Gases:
- have **no fixed shape**;
- have **no fixed volume**: they spread to fill any container;
- are usually **invisible**;
- can be squashed into a smaller space.

Examples: the air around you, the steam above a boiling pot, the gas in a cooking cylinder, the bubbles in a fizzy drink.

Air is a mix of gases. You cannot see it, but you can feel it when the wind blows, and you can see it move a flag.

**Try this:** Wave a piece of paper near your face. What do you feel?

## Three states, side by side

| | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Keeps its shape? | Yes | No | No |
| Can be poured? | No | Yes | No (it spreads) |
| Fills its container? | No | Fills the bottom | Fills all of it |
| Can you see it? | Yes | Yes | Usually no |
| Example | stone | water | air |

Here is a quick test:

1. Does it keep its shape on its own? → **Solid**.
2. Can you pour it, and does it sit in the bottom of a container? → **Liquid**.
3. Does it spread out everywhere and is hard to see? → **Gas**.

**Try this:** Use the test on these: sand, milk, smoke, a spoon, honey, the air in a tyre.

## Water in three states

Water is special. We see it in all three states every day.

- **Ice** is water as a **solid**. It keeps its shape. It is cold and hard.
- **Water** is the **liquid**. It flows. We drink it.
- **Steam** (water vapour) is water as a **gas**. It rises from a boiling pot and spreads into the air.

It is all the same stuff. Only the state is different.

Ama puts a sachet of water in the freezer. By morning it is a solid block. She leaves it in the sun. By afternoon it is liquid again. Her mother boils some for tea, and steam rises up.

**Try this:** Draw three pictures: ice, water, steam. Label each one with its state.

## Melting

**Melting** is when a solid turns into a liquid. It happens when the solid gets **warm enough**.

Put an ice cube on a plate in the sun. Slowly it gets smaller and a puddle grows around it. The ice is melting. Solid → liquid.

Other things melt too:

- Butter melts in a hot pan.
- Candle wax melts near the flame.
- Chocolate melts in your hand.
- Shea butter melts on warm skin.

Each solid has its own melting temperature. Ice melts at 0 °C. Butter needs a little more heat. Iron needs a very, very hot fire.

**Try this:** Hold a small piece of ice in your hand for one minute. What happens? Where did the heat come from?

@image https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9d/Melting_ice_block.jpg/960px-Melting_ice_block.jpg | A block of ice melting | SeppVei · Public domain | https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Melting_ice_block.jpg
===
## Freezing

**Freezing** is the opposite of melting. A liquid turns into a solid when it gets **cold enough**.

Kofi fills an ice tray with water and puts it in the freezer. The freezer takes heat away from the water. A few hours later the water is hard ice. Liquid → solid.

Water freezes at **0 °C**. That is why we call 0 °C the freezing point of water.

Other liquids freeze too, but at different temperatures. Palm oil goes solid and cloudy on a cool morning. Melted candle wax goes hard again as it cools.

Freezing does not need a freezer. It just needs the liquid to lose enough heat.

**Try this:** Watch a bottle of palm oil on a cool morning and on a hot afternoon. Describe the difference.
===
## Melting and freezing go both ways

Melting and freezing are a pair. Heat goes in: solid melts to liquid. Heat goes out: liquid freezes to solid.

Ice → (warm) → water → (cold) → ice → (warm) → water …

You can go round and round as many times as you like. The water is not used up. It only changes state.

Abena's ice-cream melts in the sun. If she puts the melted ice-cream back in the freezer, it goes hard again. It will not look as nice, but it is the same stuff.

This is a **reversible** change. It can be undone.

**Try this:** Melt a little butter in a spoon over a candle (ask an adult). Let it cool. What happens?
===
## Boiling and evaporating

A liquid can turn into a gas. This is called **evaporation**.

When water gets hot enough, it **boils**. Bubbles rise and burst, and steam rushes out. Water boils at **100 °C**. Liquid → gas.

But water does not have to boil to become a gas. Wet clothes on the line dry even on a cool day. The water slowly turns into vapour and drifts away. That is evaporation too, just slower.

Puddles dry up after rain. A wet blackboard dries. Water in an open cup slowly goes down. All evaporation.

**Try this:** Put a little water in a saucer and leave it in the sun. Check it every hour. Where did the water go?

## Condensing

A gas can turn back into a liquid. This is called **condensation**. It happens when the gas gets **cooler**.

Hold a cold metal spoon above a boiling pot (carefully). Drops of water appear on the spoon. The steam touched the cold spoon, lost heat and turned back into liquid water. Gas → liquid.

You see condensation all the time:

- Drops on the outside of a cold bottle of water. Water vapour in the air cooled on the bottle.
- A foggy mirror after a hot bath.
- Morning dew on grass.
- Clouds! They are tiny drops of water that condensed high in the cold sky.

**Try this:** Breathe on a cold window or mirror. What appears? Where did it come from?
===
## The water cycle

Evaporation and condensation drive the **water cycle**.

1. The sun heats the sea, rivers and Lake Volta. Water **evaporates** into the air as vapour.
2. The vapour rises. High up it is cold, so it **condenses** into tiny drops. These make **clouds**.
3. The drops join together and get heavy. They fall as **rain**.
4. Rain fills the rivers and the lake. The sun shines, and it starts again.

The same water has been going round for millions of years. The rain that falls on your school may once have been in the sea at Elmina.

**Try this:** Draw the water cycle as a circle with four pictures: sun on the sea, cloud, rain, river.
===
## Heat makes the change

Here is the big idea of this book. **Heat** is what changes the state of matter.

**Add heat:**
solid → liquid → gas
(melt) (evaporate)

**Take heat away:**
gas → liquid → solid
(condense) (freeze)

Ice in a pot on the fire: first it melts to water, then the water boils to steam. Steam on a cold lid: it condenses to drops, and in a freezer the drops would freeze to ice.

Nothing is created or destroyed. The water only gains or loses heat.

## Cooking is changing states

Kofi's mother is cooking. Watch the states change.

- She puts solid **shea butter** in the pan. It melts into a liquid.
- She pours in liquid **water**. It boils and steam rises as a gas.
- She drops in solid **rice**. The rice stays solid but soaks up water.
- The **steam** touches the cold lid and turns back to drops of liquid water. They drip back into the pot.

Candle wax, butter, chocolate and ice all melt at low heat. Metal pots do not melt on a cooking fire; they need much more heat. That is why they are good for cooking!

**Try this:** Next time someone cooks, list every solid, liquid and gas you notice.

## Candle wax: solid, liquid, gas

A burning candle shows all three states at once.

- The candle itself is **solid** wax.
- Near the flame, the heat **melts** the wax into a small pool of **liquid**.
- The liquid wax travels up the wick, gets very hot and turns into a **gas**. The gas is what actually burns.
- Drips that run down the side cool and **freeze** back into solid wax.

When you blow out the candle, you see a thin trail of smoke. That is wax vapour cooling in the air.

Ask an adult before you look closely at a candle. Never touch the flame or the melted wax.

**Try this:** Watch a candle for two minutes with an adult. Point to the solid wax, the liquid wax and where the gas burns.

## Sachet water: a cold experiment

Pure water sachets are everywhere in Ghana. They are perfect for an experiment.

1. Feel a sachet from the shop. The water inside is a **liquid**. It flows when you squeeze it.
2. Put it in a freezer overnight. In the morning it is a hard block: a **solid**. Notice it got slightly bigger. Water swells when it freezes!
3. Leave it on a plate in the shade. Watch drops form on the outside. That is **condensation** from the air.
4. After a few hours the block has **melted**. It is a liquid again.

You saw freezing, condensation and melting with one sachet of water.

**Try this:** Time how long the frozen sachet takes to melt in the shade. Then try one in the sun. Which is faster, and why?

## Is it a solid? Tricky cases

Some things are hard to sort.

**Sand.** You can pour sand, so is it a liquid? No. Look closely: each grain is a tiny solid that keeps its shape. Sand is many small solids.

**Powder, flour, sugar.** The same. Tiny solids.

**Sponge or foam.** Soft, but keeps its shape. A solid with air holes.

**Jelly.** Wobbly, but keeps its shape on a plate. A soft solid.

**Toothpaste.** Flows slowly out of the tube, then holds its shape on the brush. Scientists call this a very thick liquid.

Rule of thumb: look at the **smallest piece**. If each tiny piece keeps its shape, it is a solid.

**Try this:** Pour some sugar, then some water. Describe how each one moves.
===
## Is it a liquid? Thick and thin

Not all liquids flow the same way.

Water pours fast. Honey pours slowly. Palm oil is in between. Engine oil is slow and sticky.

How easily a liquid flows is called its **viscosity**. Thick liquids like honey have high viscosity. Thin liquids like water have low viscosity.

Heat changes viscosity too. Cold honey hardly moves. Warm honey runs easily. That is why shea butter is hard in the morning and soft by afternoon.

Still, all of these are liquids: they take the shape of the container and sit in the bottom.

**Try this:** Tilt a spoon of water, a spoon of honey and a spoon of palm oil. Which reaches the edge first? Put them in order from fastest to slowest.
===
## Is it a gas? Seeing the invisible

Gases are hard to notice because most are invisible. But you can prove they are there.

- **Blow up a balloon.** Something fills it. That something is gas.
- **Turn an empty cup upside down and push it into water.** The water cannot get in. The "empty" cup is full of air.
- **Open a fizzy drink.** The bubbles are gas escaping from the liquid.
- **Fan yourself.** You feel air moving on your skin.
- **Watch smoke** from a fire. The smoke is tiny solid bits carried by hot gas, so you can see which way the gas goes.

Gases have weight too. A full football is heavier than a flat one.

**Try this:** Try the upside-down cup in a bucket of water. Is the inside wet or dry when you lift it out?
===
## Air is all around us

The most important gas is the **air** we breathe. It is a mix of gases: mostly nitrogen and oxygen.

- Your lungs take oxygen from the air.
- Fire needs oxygen to burn. Cover a flame with a cup and it goes out.
- Air fills tyres, footballs and balloons.
- Wind is moving air. It pushes sails, dries clothes and turns windmills.

Air is everywhere, even in "empty" rooms, in the soil and in water (that is how fish breathe).

Air can be **squashed**. Press a football pump and you push the air into a smaller space. Liquids and solids cannot be squashed like that.

**Try this:** Hold your breath for ten seconds. What does your body want? That is how important this gas is.
===
## Steam is not smoke

People often mix these up.

**Steam** is water as a gas. It comes from boiling water. It is clean, and when it cools it turns back to water drops. You can see a white cloud above a kettle; that cloud is actually tiny drops of condensed water, not the gas itself. The real steam, right at the spout, is invisible.

**Smoke** comes from burning. It is tiny bits of solid ash and soot floating in hot gas. It is dirty and smells.

Steam from a pot of rice: safe to smell, but very hot. Smoke from a fire: do not breathe it in.

**Try this:** Look above a boiling pot. Find the clear, invisible gap just above the water before the white cloud starts.
===
## Changes you can undo

Melting, freezing, evaporating and condensing are **reversible** changes. You can go back.

Ice → water → ice. Water → steam → water.

Nothing new is made. It is still water.

Some changes are **not** reversible. When you cook an egg, it goes from runny to solid. But you cannot cool it back into a raw egg. When wood burns, it becomes ash and gas. You cannot un-burn it.

Changing state is reversible. Cooking and burning are not.

**Try this:** Sort these into "can undo" and "cannot undo": melting chocolate, frying plantain, freezing juice, burning paper, boiling water.
===
## Temperature and the thermometer

We measure how hot something is with a **thermometer**. The unit is degrees Celsius, written **°C**.

Useful numbers for water:

- **0 °C**: water freezes, ice melts.
- **100 °C**: water boils.
- About **37 °C**: your body.
- About **30 °C**: a hot afternoon in Accra.

Ice from the freezer may be below 0 °C. As it warms up it reaches 0 °C and starts to melt. It stays at 0 °C until all the ice has melted, then the water warms further.

**Try this:** If you have a thermometer, measure a cup of tap water, a cup of iced water and a cup of warm water. Write down each temperature.
===
## Keeping things cold, keeping things hot

If heat changes state, we can slow the change by keeping heat out, or in.

- **Cool box.** Thick walls stop heat getting in, so the ice inside melts slowly. Sachet sellers use them.
- **Flask.** Keeps tea hot by stopping heat escaping.
- **Shade.** The sun's heat cannot reach the ice.
- **Cloth wrap.** Wrapping a block of ice in cloth slows melting.
- **Lid on the pot.** Traps steam, so the water does not all evaporate away.

Materials that slow heat are called **insulators**: foam, cloth, wood, air gaps. Metal lets heat through quickly.

**Try this:** Put one ice cube in a cup wrapped in cloth and one in a bare cup. Which melts first?
===
## States of matter in the weather

The weather is states of matter at work.

- **Rain** is liquid water falling from clouds.
- **Clouds and fog** are tiny drops of liquid floating in the air.
- **Dew** is water that condensed on cool grass at night.
- **Harmattan dust** is tiny solids carried by dry air.
- **Hail**, which is rare in Ghana, is frozen rain: solid.
- **Humidity** is water vapour, a gas, in the air. On a sticky day there is a lot of it.

In the rainy season the air is full of water vapour. In the Harmattan the air is dry, so wet clothes dry fast and lips crack.

**Try this:** Each morning for a week, write down whether you see dew, fog, rain or dust.

## Materials and their states

A **material** is what something is made of. Most materials are solid at room temperature, but each has its own melting point.

| Material | State at room temperature | Melts at |
|---|---|---|
| Ice | solid (if kept cold) | 0 °C |
| Butter | solid | about 35 °C |
| Candle wax | solid | about 60 °C |
| Plastic bag | solid | about 120 °C |
| Iron | solid | about 1500 °C |

That is why a plastic spoon goes soft in a hot pot but an iron spoon does not.

Some materials are liquid at room temperature: water, oil, mercury in old thermometers. Some are gas: air, cooking gas.

**Try this:** Why should you never leave a plastic plate near the cooking fire?

## Experiment: the melting race

You need: three small pieces of ice the same size, three saucers.

1. Put one saucer in the sun, one in the shade, one in a fridge (if you have one).
2. Start a clock.
3. Check every five minutes. Write down which ice has melted most.

**What to expect:** the ice in the sun melts first, because it gets the most heat. The ice in the fridge melts last, or not at all.

**Write it up:**
- What I did
- What I saw
- What I learned

This is how real scientists work: a question, a fair test, careful watching, and a conclusion.
===
## Experiment: disappearing water

You need: two cups, water, a plate.

1. Pour the same amount of water into both cups.
2. Cover one cup with the plate. Leave the other open.
3. Put both in the sun for a day.

**What to expect:** the open cup has less water at the end. The covered cup has about the same, and there may be drops on the underside of the plate.

**Why:** water in the open cup evaporated into the air. In the covered cup, the vapour hit the plate, condensed and fell back.

**Try this:** Mark the water level with a pen at the start so you can measure the change.
===
## Melting is not dissolving

People sometimes say sugar "melts" in tea. It does not. It **dissolves**.

**Melting** needs heat and changes a solid into a liquid. Ice melts into water. Nothing else is needed.

**Dissolving** needs a liquid to mix into. Sugar in water breaks into tiny bits too small to see and spreads through the water. The water tastes sweet, but the sugar is still sugar.

Test: a sugar cube on a warm plate stays a cube. A sugar cube in warm water disappears. The first is not melting, because sugar needs a much hotter oven to melt. The second is dissolving.

Salt, sugar and Milo dissolve. Sand and stones do not.

**Try this:** Stir a spoon of salt into a cup of water. Can you see it? Can you taste it? Where did it go?
===
## Experiment: the balloon and the bottle

Gases spread out when they get warm. Here is a way to see it.

You need: an empty plastic bottle, a balloon, a bowl of hot water (ask an adult), a bowl of cold water.

1. Stretch the balloon over the mouth of the empty bottle. The bottle looks empty, but it is full of air.
2. Stand the bottle in the hot water. Wait. The balloon slowly **rises** and starts to fill.
3. Move the bottle to the cold water. The balloon **sinks** again.

**Why:** when the air inside warms up, it spreads out and needs more room, so it pushes into the balloon. When it cools, it shrinks back.

Nothing was added to the bottle. Only heat went in and out.

**Try this:** Draw the bottle in hot water and in cold water. Label the air in each one.

## Safety with heat

Changing states often needs heat, and heat can hurt.

- **Steam burns.** It is hotter than boiling water. Never put your hand over a steaming pot.
- **Melted wax and hot oil burn.** Keep away from the pan.
- **Ice can hurt too.** Very cold ice sticks to wet skin. Do not hold it for a long time.
- **Gas cylinders** must be turned off after cooking and kept away from flames.
- **Never** do heating experiments without an adult.

A good scientist is a careful scientist.

## What you have learned

You can now:

- Say that all matter is a **solid**, a **liquid** or a **gas**.
- Describe each state: solids keep their shape, liquids flow and take the shape of their container, gases spread out to fill the space.
- Explain **melting** (solid → liquid), **freezing** (liquid → solid), **evaporating** (liquid → gas) and **condensing** (gas → liquid).
- Say that **heat** going in or out causes these changes, and that they are **reversible**.
- Give everyday examples: ice, sachet water, shea butter, candle wax, steam from the pot.
- Describe the **water cycle**.

Next you will learn about mixing materials and dissolving. The same careful watching will help you there.
