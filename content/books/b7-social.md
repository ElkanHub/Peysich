## What is a map?

A **map** is a drawing of a place seen from above, made smaller so it fits on a page or a screen. It shows where things are and how far apart they are.

Imagine a bird flying over your school. It sees the roofs of the classrooms, the football field, the path to the gate, the road outside. If the bird could draw what it saw, that drawing would be a map.

Maps are old. People scratched them on clay and drew them on animal skin thousands of years ago. Today they are also on phones, in trotros, on the walls of the district assembly and in your social studies textbook.

Maps answer questions:

- Where is Tamale compared to Accra?
- Which road goes from Kumasi to Sunyani?
- How far is the clinic from the school?
- Which regions share a border with the Volta Region?

To answer these questions, a map uses a set of tools: a **title**, a **key**, a **scale**, a **compass** and often a **grid**. This book teaches you each one, and then you will use them on the map of Ghana and your own district.
===
## The parts of a map

Almost every good map has the same five parts. Learn to find them first, before you look at anything else.

@image https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8e/Ghana_Regions_map.png/960px-Ghana_Regions_map.png | A map of Ghana with a title (bottom right), a scale bar (bottom), a compass (right) and colours and symbols explained by the map's key | Burmesedays · CC BY-SA 3.0 | https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ghana_Regions_map.png

**1. Title.** Tells you what the map shows. On the map above the title is simply "Ghana".

**2. Key (or legend).** Explains the colours, lines and symbols. A yellow line might mean a main road; a blue square might mean a national park.

**3. Scale.** Tells you how much smaller the map is than real life, so you can measure distances. On this map, a short bar shows how long 50 km is.

**4. Compass.** Shows which way is north. On this map it is the arrow at the bottom right.

**5. Grid** (not on every map). Lines that divide the map into squares so you can describe exactly where something is.

Together these are sometimes remembered as **TKSCG**, or you can make your own memory sentence.

## Kinds of maps

Not all maps show the same things. Before you read one, work out which kind it is. The title usually tells you.

**Political maps** show boundaries: countries, regions, districts, and their capitals. The regions map of Ghana is political. Colours separate one region from another and have no other meaning.

**Physical (relief) maps** show the land itself: mountains, plateaus, lowlands, rivers and lakes. Colours mean height.

**Road maps** show roads of different sizes, towns and distances. Drivers use them. The thick lines are the trunk roads; the thin ones are feeder roads.

**Street maps** show one town at a large scale: every street, with names, plus landmarks like markets, churches, mosques and lorry parks.

**Thematic maps** show one topic laid over the outline of a place: rainfall, population, crops, languages, diseases, election results. The key explains the shading.

**Sketch maps** are quick hand-drawn maps, like the one you draw for a friend to find your house. Not to scale, but still useful.

**Try this**
For each question, say which kind of map you would need: Where does cocoa grow? Which road goes to Ho? Where is the Tamale lorry park? Which regions border Ashanti?

## Maps through history

People have made maps for thousands of years. The oldest known maps were scratched on clay tablets in Babylon over 2 500 years ago. Ancient Egyptians drew maps of the Nile on papyrus.

For most of history, map-makers (called **cartographers**) worked from travellers' stories and their own measurements. Coastlines were drawn fairly well because sailors knew them. Inland areas were often guessed or left blank.

**Maps of Ghana.** European traders drew the Gold Coast from the 1400s onwards, marking the forts at Elmina, Cape Coast and Accra. These maps were made for trade and often ignored the towns and kingdoms inland. In the 1900s the colonial Survey Department made the first detailed maps of the whole country, measuring it piece by piece on foot. After independence, the **Survey and Mapping Division** of the Lands Commission took over, and Ghana produced its own national map series.

**Today.** Satellites photograph the whole Earth every few days. GPS satellites let a phone fix its position to within a few metres. Volunteers add roads and buildings to free online maps. Yet the rules of reading a map, its title, key, scale, compass and grid, are the same as they were on clay.

## The title

The **title** is the first thing to read. It tells you what the map is about and saves you from guessing.

Compare these titles:

- *Ghana: Regions*
- *Ghana: Rainfall*
- *Ghana: Main Roads*
- *Accra Metropolitan Area: Schools*

All four maps might show the same outline of the country or city. But they tell completely different stories. On the rainfall map, the colours mean millimetres of rain. On the regions map, the colours mean regions. If you did not read the title, you might think the Western Region had more rain than the Upper East simply because it was a darker colour.

A good title also tells you **when**. A map titled *Regions of Ghana (2018)* shows ten regions. A map titled *Regions of Ghana (2019)* shows sixteen. Both are correct for their date.

**Try this**
Look at any map in your textbook or classroom. Write down its title. Then write one sentence saying what questions that map can answer and one question it cannot answer.

## The key

The **key** (also called the **legend**) is the map's dictionary. It lists every colour, line and symbol used on the map and says what each one means.

Maps use symbols because there is no room for words. Instead of writing "hospital" fifty times, a map puts a small cross and explains it once in the key.

Common symbols:

- A thick red or yellow line: main road
- A thin line: minor road or track
- A black line with cross-bars: railway
- Blue lines and areas: rivers, lakes, the sea
- Green: forest or park
- A small cross: hospital or clinic
- A small aeroplane: airport
- A dot or circle: town or village (bigger dot, bigger town)
- A star: capital city

**Worked example**
On the Ghana map, you see a yellow line running from Kumasi to Tamale. You check the key: yellow means main road. So there is a main road between the two cities. You see a blue square near Wa labelled "Mole NP". The key says blue square = national park. So Mole National Park is near Wa.

Always look at the key **before** you try to read the map.

## Three kinds of symbol

Every symbol in a key is one of three kinds. Knowing this helps you read unfamiliar maps quickly.

**Point symbols** mark a single place. A dot for a village, a cross for a clinic, a small tent for a campsite, a star for a capital city. The symbol sits exactly where the thing is. The size of a point symbol often tells you size in real life: a big circle for a city, a small one for a village.

**Line symbols** mark things that are long and thin. Roads, railways, rivers, footpaths, boundaries and power lines are all drawn as lines. The style of line matters: thick for a main road, thin for a track, dashed for a boundary, blue for a river. Lines on a map are usually drawn wider than the real thing, or a road would be too thin to see.

**Area symbols** fill a space with colour or pattern. Green for forest, blue for a lake, grey for a built-up town, a pattern of small trees for a plantation. The edge of the colour is the edge of the area.

**Worked example**
On the Ghana map: Mole National Park is an **area** symbol (a blue square used as a marker). The Black Volta is a **line**. Bolgatanga is a **point**.

List five things in your town and say which kind of symbol each would need.

## The compass: the four main directions

A **compass** shows direction. The compass on a map tells you which way is **north**. Once you know north, you know all the others.

@image https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f3/CC0_Compass_Rose.svg/960px-CC0_Compass_Rose.svg.png | A compass rose. The long points are north, east, south and west; the shorter points are the directions in between | Kertase · CC0 | https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:CC0_Compass_Rose.svg

The four main directions are called the **cardinal points**:

- **North (N)** – at the top of most maps
- **East (E)** – on the right
- **South (S)** – at the bottom
- **West (W)** – on the left

Going clockwise from the top: N, E, S, W. A well-known memory sentence is **"Never Eat Soggy Waakye"**.

In real life, the sun rises roughly in the east and sets roughly in the west. If you stand facing where the sun rises, north is on your left and south is on your right.

On the map of Ghana: the sea is to the **south**. Burkina Faso is to the **north**. Togo is to the **east**. Côte d'Ivoire is to the **west**.

## The compass: the in-between directions

Between the four cardinal points are four more. They are called the **intercardinal** or **ordinal** points.

- **North-east (NE)** – between north and east
- **South-east (SE)** – between south and east
- **South-west (SW)** – between south and west
- **North-west (NW)** – between north and west

Notice that north and south always come first in the name: north-east, not east-north.

With eight directions you can describe almost any position.

**Worked example**
Using a map of Ghana:
- Tamale is **north** of Kumasi.
- Ho is **east** of Accra (and a little north).
- Takoradi is **south-west** of Kumasi.
- Bolgatanga is **north-east** of Wa.
- Cape Coast is **west** of Accra.

**How to work it out:** put your finger on the starting place. Imagine the compass rose sitting on it. Which arm points towards the other place? That is the direction.

**Try this**
Using the compass, give the direction from your classroom to: the school gate, the headteacher's office, the nearest tap. Check by finding where the sun rises.

## Recap: title, key and compass

Three of the five map tools are done. Here is what to remember.

**Title** – read it first. It tells you what the map shows and often when.

**Key (legend)** – the map's dictionary. Check it before reading the map, so you know what every colour, line and symbol means.

**Compass** – shows north. The four cardinal points are N, E, S, W (Never Eat Soggy Waakye). The four in-between points are NE, SE, SW, NW. North or south is always said first.

**Finding direction:** put the compass rose on the starting point and see which arm points to the destination.

**Ghana's neighbours by direction:** Burkina Faso to the north, Togo to the east, the Gulf of Guinea to the south, Côte d'Ivoire to the west.

## Using a real compass

Maps show north, but how do you find north when you are standing in a field?

@image https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a2/Hand_holding_Compass_against_clouds.jpg/960px-Hand_holding_Compass_against_clouds.jpg | A magnetic compass. The red end of the needle always swings to point north | Rumagoso · CC BY 2.5 | https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hand_holding_Compass_against_clouds.jpg

A **magnetic compass** has a small needle that is a magnet. The Earth itself is a giant magnet, so the needle swings until its red (or marked) end points north.

**How to use one**
1. Hold the compass flat in your palm, away from phones, keys and metal.
2. Wait for the needle to settle.
3. Turn the dial until the N lines up with the red end of the needle.
4. Now the dial shows all the directions correctly. Read off the one you need.

**Lining up a map**
Lay the map flat. Put the compass on it. Turn the map until the map's north arrow points the same way as the needle. Now the map matches the land: what is on your left on the map is on your left in real life.

Many phones have a compass app that works the same way. Farmers, fishermen, surveyors and soldiers all use compasses.
===
## Scale: making the world fit on a page

A map is smaller than the place it shows. The **scale** tells you how much smaller.

On the Ghana map, the country is about 670 km from the coast to the northern border. On the page it is about 13 cm. Everything has been shrunk by the same amount. That is what scale means: **every distance on the map has been shrunk by the same number**.

Scale can be shown in three ways:

**1. A scale bar (linear scale).** A ruler drawn on the map, marked in kilometres. You measure a distance on the map and hold it against the bar.

**2. A ratio.** For example **1 : 50 000**. This means 1 cm on the map is 50 000 cm in real life, which is 500 m.

**3. A statement.** For example "1 cm to 5 km".

@image https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/dc/Map_scale_-_8km%2C_5mi.png | A scale bar. The top edge is marked in kilometres, the bottom in miles | Anna Frodesiak · CC0 | https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Map_scale_-_8km,_5mi.png

A **large-scale** map shows a small area in great detail (your town). A **small-scale** map shows a large area with little detail (all of Africa).
===
## Measuring distance with a scale bar

The scale bar is the easiest way to measure distance. Here is the method.

**Straight-line distance**
1. Lay the edge of a strip of paper between the two places on the map.
2. Mark both places on the paper.
3. Move the paper to the scale bar, putting one mark at zero.
4. Read off how many kilometres the other mark reaches.

**Worked example**
On a map with a scale bar where 2 cm = 50 km, you measure Kumasi to Sunyani as 4 cm.
4 cm is twice 2 cm, so the distance is twice 50 km = **100 km**. (The real road is longer because it bends.)

**Distance along a winding road**
Roads bend, so you cannot use a straight edge. Instead:
1. Put the edge of the paper at the start.
2. Follow the road a short way until it bends, mark the paper, then turn the paper to follow the next straight piece.
3. Keep going, adding each short straight section.
4. Measure the total against the scale bar.

A piece of thread laid along the road and then straightened works too.

## Working with a ratio scale

Ratio scales need a little arithmetic. Follow the steps and they are not hard.

A scale of **1 : 100 000** means 1 unit on the map = 100 000 of the same units on the ground.

So 1 cm on the map = 100 000 cm on the ground.
100 000 cm = 1 000 m = **1 km**.

So on a 1 : 100 000 map, **1 cm = 1 km**. Easy to remember.

**Other common scales**
- 1 : 50 000 → 1 cm = 500 m (half a kilometre)
- 1 : 25 000 → 1 cm = 250 m
- 1 : 1 000 000 → 1 cm = 10 km

**Worked example**
On a 1 : 50 000 map, the clinic is 6 cm from the school. Real distance?
1 cm = 500 m, so 6 cm = 6 × 500 = **3 000 m = 3 km**.

**Worked example 2**
On a 1 : 1 000 000 map, Accra to Kumasi measures 20 cm. Real distance?
1 cm = 10 km, so 20 cm = **200 km**.

**Try this**
1. 1 : 25 000, distance 8 cm. Real distance?
2. 1 : 100 000, distance 15 cm. Real distance?
3. On a 1 : 50 000 map, how many cm would show a 7 km road?

## Choosing the right scale

Different jobs need different scales. Before you pick a map, ask: how much area do I need, and how much detail?

**Large scale (e.g. 1 : 10 000)** shows a small area, big. You can see individual buildings, footpaths, the school compound, the market stalls. Good for: finding a house, planning a new drain, drawing your school.

**Medium scale (e.g. 1 : 50 000)** shows a district. You see towns, main roads, rivers, hills. Good for: planning a journey, locating a village, seeing which farms are near a river.

**Small scale (e.g. 1 : 5 000 000)** shows a whole country or continent, small. You see regions, cities, big rivers. Good for: comparing regions, learning where places are.

**Worked example**
Ama is giving directions to her house in Madina for a delivery. Should she draw a large-scale or small-scale map?
Large scale. The driver needs to see streets and landmarks, not the whole of Accra.

Kofi wants to show where Ghana's cocoa grows. Which scale?
Small scale, a map of the whole country, with the cocoa areas shaded.

**Try this**
Which scale would you choose for a map of (a) the route from your classroom to the toilets, (b) the sixteen regions, (c) your district's health centres?

## Recap: scale

- **Scale** tells you how much smaller the map is than the real place.
- Three ways to show it: a **scale bar**, a **ratio** (1 : 50 000), or a **statement** (1 cm to 500 m).
- To measure with a **scale bar**: mark the distance on a strip of paper, then read it against the bar.
- For winding roads, follow the road in short straight steps, or use a thread.
- With a **ratio**: 1 : 100 000 means 1 cm = 1 km. 1 : 50 000 means 1 cm = 500 m. Multiply the map distance by what 1 cm stands for.
- **Large scale** = small area, lots of detail. **Small scale** = big area, little detail.

**Quick check**
1. On a 1 : 50 000 map, a river is 10 cm long. Real length?
2. A scale bar shows 3 cm = 30 km. Two towns are 7.5 cm apart. Distance?
3. Which has the larger scale, a map of Kumasi or a map of West Africa?
4. Why is the real road distance usually longer than the straight-line distance you measure?

## Grids: dividing the map into squares

When someone says "the clinic is in the middle of the map, a bit to the left", that is not very helpful. A **grid** solves this. It divides the map into squares, and each square has an address.

A grid is a set of lines drawn across the map. Vertical lines run north to south. Horizontal lines run east to west. Where they cross, they make squares.

The simplest grids use **letters along one side and numbers along the other**, like a game board. A square is named by its letter and number: B3, D7.

Many street maps use this system. The index lists "Makola Market … C4", and you look across to column C and down to row 4.

**Worked example**
On a school map with a letter-number grid, the football field is in square C2 and the library is in A5. Which is further north (towards the top), if the rows are numbered from the top?
Row 2 is nearer the top than row 5, so the football field is further north.

**Try this**
Draw a 6 × 6 grid on squared paper. Label columns A to F and rows 1 to 6. Draw five things from your school compound in different squares and write their grid addresses.
===
## Four-figure grid references

Proper survey maps use **numbers along both sides**. The vertical lines are called **eastings** (the numbers get bigger as you go east). The horizontal lines are called **northings** (bigger as you go north).

A **four-figure grid reference** names one whole square. The rule is:

**Along the corridor, then up the stairs.**

1. Find the vertical line on the **left** edge of the square. Read its number (the easting). That is the first two figures.
2. Find the horizontal line on the **bottom** edge of the square. Read its number (the northing). That is the last two figures.

So a square whose bottom-left corner is at easting 23 and northing 45 has the grid reference **2345**.

**Worked example**
A church sits in the square whose left line is 17 and whose bottom line is 62. Grid reference: **1762**.

**Common mistake:** reading the northing first. Always eastings (across) first, then northings (up). Corridor, then stairs.

## Six-figure grid references

A four-figure reference gives a square that might be a whole kilometre wide. For something small, like a borehole, we need to be more exact. A **six-figure grid reference** points to a spot inside the square.

Imagine each square divided into ten strips across and ten strips up. The extra figures tell you how many tenths across and up the point is.

**Method**
1. Find the four-figure reference of the square as before: easting, then northing.
2. Estimate how many tenths across the square the point is (0 to 9). Put this figure after the easting.
3. Estimate how many tenths up the square the point is. Put this figure after the northing.

**Worked example**
A borehole is in square 2345. It is about 7 tenths across and 3 tenths up.
- Easting: 23, then 7 → 237
- Northing: 45, then 3 → 453
Grid reference: **237453**.

A six-figure reference pins a place to within 100 m on a 1 : 50 000 map. That is why rescue teams, surveyors and the army use them.

**Try this**
In square 1762, a well is 2 tenths across and 8 tenths up. Six-figure reference? (Answer: 172628.)

## Worked example: directions on a street map

Kofi is new in Cape Coast. He has a street map and must get from the lorry park to the hospital. Here is how he uses the map.

**Step 1: Find both places.** He uses the index: "Lorry park … D3" and "Regional Hospital … B5". He finds both squares.

**Step 2: Work out the general direction.** From D3 to B5 means moving left (west) two columns and down (south) two rows. So the hospital is roughly **south-west** of the lorry park.

**Step 3: Find a route.** He looks for roads (line symbols) joining the two squares. A main road runs south from D3 to D5, then another runs west from D5 to B5.

**Step 4: Measure.** With a strip of paper along the two straight parts, he gets 6 cm. The scale bar shows 2 cm = 500 m, so the walk is about 1 500 m, or **1.5 km**, about twenty minutes.

**Step 5: Note landmarks.** The key shows a church symbol at the corner where he must turn west. He will know the turn when he sees the church.

**Step 6: Line up the map.** Outside, he turns the map so its north arrow points north (the sea is south, so he puts the sea side towards the sea). Now "left on the map" is left in real life.

Write similar steps for a visitor going from your school to the nearest market.

## Ghana and the Prime Meridian

Besides grids, the whole Earth has its own system of lines: **latitude** and **longitude**.

**Lines of latitude** run east to west around the Earth, parallel to the **Equator** (0°). They measure how far north or south a place is. Ghana lies between about **5°N** (the coast) and **11°N** (the northern border). It is entirely in the northern hemisphere, close to the Equator, which is why it is hot all year.

**Lines of longitude** run from the North Pole to the South Pole. They measure how far east or west a place is from the **Prime Meridian** (0°), the line that passes through Greenwich in London.

Here is something special: the Prime Meridian passes through **Ghana**. It crosses the coast at **Tema**, just east of Accra, and runs north through the Volta Region and up to the Burkina Faso border. Ghana is one of only a handful of countries on the 0° line. This is also why Ghana's time is **Greenwich Mean Time (GMT)**.

So most of Ghana is slightly **west** of 0° (Kumasi is about 1.6°W, Wa about 2.5°W), and a thin strip in the east is slightly **east** of 0° (Ho is about 0.5°E).

## Recap: grids and references

- A **grid** divides a map into squares so each place has an address.
- Simple maps use **letters and numbers** (C4). Survey maps use **numbers on both edges**.
- **Eastings** are the vertical lines; the numbers grow as you go east. **Northings** are the horizontal lines; the numbers grow as you go north.
- **Four-figure reference** = one square. Read the easting on the left of the square, then the northing on the bottom. **Along the corridor, then up the stairs.**
- **Six-figure reference** = a point in the square. Add tenths across after the easting and tenths up after the northing.
- Example: square 2345, 7 tenths across, 3 tenths up → 237453.

**Quick check**
1. Which comes first in a grid reference, easting or northing?
2. What is the four-figure reference of the square whose bottom-left corner is at easting 08, northing 91?
3. In that square, a point 5 tenths across and 5 tenths up. Six-figure reference?
4. Why would a fire service want a six-figure reference instead of a four-figure one?

## The map of Ghana: sixteen regions

Since 2019 Ghana has had **sixteen regions**. Each has a capital town where the Regional Minister works.

## The map of Ghana: relief

A **relief map** (or physical map) shows the shape of the land: where it is high, where it is low, where rivers run.

What the map shows:

- The highest land is the **Akwapim-Togo Ranges** along the eastern border, where Mount Afadja (885 m) stands.
- The **Kwahu Plateau** forms a curved ridge across the middle of the country.
- **Lake Volta** fills a huge area in the east. The Black Volta and White Volta rivers flow into it from the north.
- The coast is mostly flat, with lagoons near Keta and Accra.

## Travelling across Ghana with a map

Let us plan a journey using every map skill.

**Journey:** Kofi travels from Accra to Tamale to visit his grandmother.

**Direction.** Put the compass rose on Accra. Tamale is almost straight **north**, slightly west.

**Distance.** On a map with scale 1 : 5 000 000 (1 cm = 50 km), Accra to Tamale measures about 12.5 cm in a straight line. 12.5 × 50 = about **625 km**. By road it is further, roughly 650 km, because the road passes through Kumasi.

**Route.** Using the key, Kofi finds the main road (a thick line) from Accra north through Nsawam and Nkawkaw to Kumasi, then on through Techiman and Kintampo to Tamale.

**Regions passed through.** Greater Accra → Eastern → Ashanti → Bono East → Savannah → Northern.

**Landmarks.** The road crosses the Kwahu Plateau near Nkawkaw (steep, winding) and passes Kintampo, where the waterfalls are.

**Time.** A bus at about 60 km/h would take 650 ÷ 60 ≈ 11 hours, plus stops.

## Your district

Ghana's regions are divided into **districts**, more than 260 of them. Each district has a **District Assembly** and a **District Chief Executive**. Some larger ones are called **Municipal** or **Metropolitan** assemblies. Your school, your clinic and your market are all run with help from your district.

A district map is a **large-scale** map. It shows:

- the **district boundary** (usually a thick dashed line)
- the **district capital** (where the assembly meets)
- towns and villages
- roads, with the main ones marked clearly
- rivers and streams
- schools, health centres, markets
- sometimes farmland, forest reserves and mining areas

## Worked example: reading a district map

Abena has a map of her district. Here is how she reads it, step by step.

**1. Title.** "Ajumako-Enyan-Essiam District, Central Region". So she knows the district and the region.

**2. Key.** She finds: thick red = trunk road; thin red = feeder road; black cross = health facility; blue = rivers; dashed line = district boundary.

**3. Compass.** North is at the top.

**4. Scale.** 1 : 150 000, so 1 cm = 1.5 km.

**5. Finding her town.** She finds Ajumako, the district capital, near the centre.

**6. Questions she can now answer:**
- *Which direction is Essiam from Ajumako?* She lays the compass rose on Ajumako. Essiam is to the **south-west**.
- *How far is the nearest hospital?* She finds the cross symbol, measures 5 cm, and calculates 5 × 1.5 = **7.5 km**.
- *Which towns are on the trunk road?* She follows the thick red line and lists them.
- *Which river forms part of the boundary?* She follows the dashed line until it runs along a blue line.

## Drawing a map of your school

Making a map is the best way to understand one. Follow these steps to map your school compound.

**1. Walk and observe.** Walk around the compound. Note the buildings, the field, the gate, trees, water points, paths.

**2. Pace it out.** Count your steps along the longest side of the compound and the shortest side. Measure one of your steps with a ruler so you can convert.

**3. Choose a scale.** If the compound is 100 m long and your paper is 20 cm, use 1 cm = 5 m (1 : 500).

**4. Draw the outline** of the compound to scale.

**5. Add the features** in the right places and roughly the right sizes. Use simple symbols: a rectangle for a classroom block, a circle for a tree, a small triangle for a tap.

**6. Add the five tools:** a **title** ("Map of … JHS"), a **key** explaining your symbols, a **scale** statement or bar, a **compass** arrow (find north with the sunrise or a phone), and a **grid** of letters and numbers.

**7. Test it.** Give it to a friend and ask them to find the headteacher's office using only your map.
===
## Maps on phones

Most maps people use today are on a **phone**. They follow the same rules as paper maps, but with some differences.

**The same:**
- There is a **key**, though it is often hidden in a menu or the symbols are explained when you tap them.
- There is a **scale bar**, usually in the corner, which changes when you zoom.
- **North is at the top**, unless you turn on "rotate with direction".
- Rivers are blue, parks are green, main roads are thick.

**Different:**
- You **zoom** instead of choosing a scale. Zooming in makes the scale larger; zooming out makes it smaller.
- A blue dot shows **where you are**, using satellite signals (GPS).
- You can **search** instead of using a grid reference.
- The map can **give directions** and estimate travel time.

**Be careful.** Phone maps can be wrong or out of date, especially for footpaths and small villages. Rural roads may be missing. And a phone battery can die. Knowing how to read a paper map is still a skill worth having.

**Try this**
If you can borrow a phone, open a map, find your school, and zoom in and out while watching the scale bar change.

## Common mistakes

**Reading the map before the key.** You assume blue means river, but on this map it means a district boundary. Always check the key first.

**Northings before eastings.** Grid reference 4523 is a different square from 2345. Corridor first, then stairs.

**Adding instead of multiplying scale.** On 1 : 50 000, 4 cm is 4 × 500 m = 2 km, not "4 + 500".

**Forgetting that roads bend.** A straight-line measurement is always shorter than the road.

**Mixing up large and small scale.** Large scale = small area, big detail (your town). Small scale = big area, little detail (Africa). The words feel backwards, so learn them carefully.

**Assuming north is always at the top.** It usually is, but check the compass arrow. Some maps are turned to fit the page.

**East and west swapped.** Facing north, east is on your **right**. Think of the sun rising on your right hand as you face north.

**Using an old map.** A map from 2015 shows ten regions. Check the date in the title.

**Trusting one map for everything.** A roads map will not show rainfall. A rainfall map will not show schools. Choose the map for the question.

## Why map skills matter

Reading a map is not just for exams. Many jobs in Ghana depend on it every day.

**Surveyors** measure land and draw the maps that settle who owns which plot. Land disputes are common, and a good map prevents them.

**Town planners** at the district assembly use maps to decide where new roads, schools and drains should go.

**Drivers** of long-distance buses, trucks and trotros need to know routes, distances and alternatives when a road is closed.

**Health workers** map where cases of cholera or malaria appear, to find the source and stop the spread.

**NADMO** (the National Disaster Management Organisation) uses maps to plan for floods: which areas are low, which roads will be cut off, where people can shelter.

**Farmers and agricultural officers** map soils, rainfall and crops to advise what to plant where.

**Miners, foresters, fishermen, pilots, soldiers, police and delivery riders** all read maps.

And every citizen benefits: knowing where your district boundary runs, where the nearest clinic is, and how to get to a new place without getting lost.

## Key words

**Map** – a drawing of a place from above, made smaller.
**Title** – tells you what and when the map shows.
**Key / legend** – explains the colours, lines and symbols.
**Symbol** – a small picture or shape standing for a real thing.
**Scale** – how much smaller the map is than real life.
**Scale bar** – a drawn ruler marked in kilometres.
**Ratio scale** – e.g. 1 : 50 000, 1 cm on the map = 50 000 cm on the ground.
**Large scale / small scale** – small area with detail / large area with little detail.
**Compass rose** – the drawing that shows north and the other directions.
**Cardinal points** – N, E, S, W.
**Intercardinal (ordinal) points** – NE, SE, SW, NW.
**Grid** – lines dividing the map into squares.
**Eastings** – vertical grid lines, numbered increasing eastwards.
**Northings** – horizontal grid lines, numbered increasing northwards.
**Four-figure grid reference** – names a square.
**Six-figure grid reference** – names a point inside a square.
**Relief map** – shows the height and shape of the land.
**Region / district** – Ghana's levels of local government. Sixteen regions; over 260 districts.
